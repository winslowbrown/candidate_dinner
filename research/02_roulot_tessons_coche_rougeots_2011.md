# WINE (a): DOMAINE ROULOT — MEURSAULT "LES TESSONS, CLOS DE MON PLAISIR" 2011

## Headline talking points (30 seconds)

- Tessons is a ~5 ha east-facing village climat *above and directly overlooking the village of Meursault*, on the north side of the appellation, at roughly the same altitude as the lower band of the great southern premier crus. It was ranked as a "première cuvée" by Dr Lavalle (1855) and "deuxième cuvée" by Rodier (1920), and is the consensus best village climat of Meursault together with Rougeots and Chevalières. [HIGH]
- Roulot's 0.85 ha walled clos ("Clos de Mon Plaisir", now labelled "À Mon Plaisir, Clos du Haut Tesson") was planted 1951/1961 and is the domaine's flagship village wine — the one Jean-Marc calls "the most representative of the village Meursaults." [HIGH]
- 2011 was a full-crop, early-picked, clean vintage that Roulot treats as his yield reference year; Jean-Marc told Sarah Marsh MW in 2024: "I prefer it to my 2010, which had botrytis. 2011 is a great vintage." Critics were lukewarm on release (Tanzer 91, Burghound 92) and warmer in retrospect ("aged better than initially expected"). [HIGH]
- Winemaking: light foulage then very low-pressure pressing (75% of juice at 0.2 bar), one-night settling with lots of lees, indigenous ferment in barrel, ~15–20% new oak (Damy), ~11–12 months in barrel with light bâtonnage only until malo, then 6–7 months in stainless steel on lees (the "reductive" phase), bottled ~18 months after harvest, typically Feb–April. [HIGH]
- The "Dominique Lafon interregnum" in the brief is, per every source I found, actually a Ted Lemon (1982–84) / Franck Grux (1984–88) interregnum. Dominique Lafon's role was as a stylistic influence on Jean-Marc, not as Roulot's winemaker. [HIGH on Lemon/Grux; Lafon-as-winemaker NOT FOUND]

## 1. The vineyard / climat: Les Tessons (Le Tesson)

### Location, exposure, altitude, size

- **Position.** Les Tessons sits on the hillside *immediately above the village of Meursault*, on the northern (Volnay-facing) half of the commune, i.e. on the opposite side of the village from the southern premier-cru band (Perrières/Charmes/Genevrières). Patrick Essa (vigneron at Buisson-Charles, whose own Tessons parcel is a neighbour of Roulot's) describes it as a "lieu-dit surplombant le village de Meursault" whose regular, moderate slope faces "directly the village church steeple." [HIGH — Essa/degustateurs.pro 2019; winehog 2017; Marsh/WFW 2024]
- **Exposure.** Full east. Jean-Marc Roulot to Sarah Marsh (WFW, Mar 2024): "Tessons is the most representative of the village Meursaults. It faces full east, while Luchets, for example, turns slightly to face north." Essa: "orienté entièrement à l'Est avec une remarquable régularité d'orientation." [HIGH]
- **Altitude.** Sarah Marsh gives 260 m (850 ft). Winehog (Steen Öhman) says the five top northern village climats (Tesson, Rougeot, Chevalières, Grands Charrons, Petits Charrons) lie "at almost the same altitude as the 1er crus south of the village," and that Le Tesson sits "at approximately Perrières' lower elevation." Essa places Meursault's premier crus at 260–300 m. [HIGH that Tessons is mid-slope at ~260 m; MED on exact figure]
- **Size.** Lavalle (1855) lists Les Tessons at 5.29 ha; Essa (2019) says "slightly over five hectares, divided among nine proprietors." Sarah Marsh's "4 hectares" for the climat is the outlier. I trust Essa/Lavalle (5.29 ha) — Essa farms there and cites the cadastre. [HIGH ≈5.3 ha]
- **Physical features.** The climat is studded with stone vigneron huts (cabottes). Marsh: "rather grand vignerons' huts scattered across its east-facing slope"; Essa: "cabottes de quatre heures" (huts for the 4 o'clock break). [HIGH]

### Soil / geology

- Essa: "Terres rougeoyantes et ferrugineuses, chargées en oxyde de fer mêlée de calcaire" — reddish, ferruginous soil charged with iron oxide mixed with limestone; argilo-calcaire substrate "marked by small stones and meagre earth." [HIGH]
- Kermit Lynch tech sheet: "clay and limestone." [HIGH, generic]
- Charles Curtis MW (Decanter, 2024) groups Rougeots, Chevalières, Tessons, Grands/Petits Charrons as the sector "exposed to the Ruisseau des Cloux valley cooling influence" — i.e. the combe that cuts through the north of the village brings cooler air onto these slopes. [MED]
- William Kelley ("What is Meursault?", 2016) notes that Meursault's east-facing hillside village vineyards sit at the same altitude as Puligny's premier and grand crus yet carry only village status, and that the lower vineyards below 250 m benefit from a water table "considerably lower than that of the village's southern neighbours." [HIGH]

### Name etymology

- NOT FOUND from a primary toponymy source in this session. The commonly repeated derivation (French *tesson* = potsherd / fragment of tile or stone, alluding to the stony, broken surface) is plausible but I could not source it; do not assert it as fact. Essa's Tessons page (which discusses etymology) was blocked on fetch. [LOW]

### Classification history and near-misses

- **Lavalle 1855**: Les Tessons ranked *Première Cuvée* — winehog quotes "Les Tessons (5.29 ha)" among premier-level sites. Essa concurs: "Positionné en première classe au 19ième siècle par le Dr Lavalle." [HIGH]
- **1862 classification**: Le Tesson in *2e classe* alongside Petits-Charrons, Chevalières, Rougeots, Grands-Charrons. [HIGH — winehog]
- **Rodier 1920**: Le Tesson a *Deuxième Cuvée* with Rougeots, Grands-Charrons, Petits-Charrons, Chevalières. [HIGH]
- **AOC era (1936–37 onward)**: village only. Marsh (2024): Tessons "was historically rated as deuxième cru before the current AOC classification system. Roulot considers it 'of premier cru quality' but declines to challenge the official classification." Essa: "c'est un cru qui est chaque année très proche des premiers crus." [HIGH]
- **Why Meursault has no grand cru.** Curtis (Decanter): Perrières is "grand cru in all but name," and elevation efforts failed due to "fragmented ownership." Essa's framing is different and worth having at table: the commune, classified in the early 1930s, "rejected Grand Cru classification in favour of emphasising the unified 'Meursault' appellation name," preferring a balanced pyramid of 1er cru / village / regional. Raphaël Coche's line (Curtis, Decanter 2022): "Tout est sous-classé à Meursault" — everything in Meursault is under-classified. [MED — two different explanations; both are period folklore; Essa's is the one told in the village]
- Winehog's caveat on the historic rankings: 19th-century rankings are biased toward lower-slope sites because some upper slopes were planted to red or not fully developed, and climate change has since raised the quality of the higher village climats (Tillets, Narvaux, Luchets). [MED]

### Neighbours and how Tessons differs

- Immediately **north/below** Tessons: Les Rougeots (see Wine (b)). Winehog: "Meursault Les Rougeot is located just north of Les Tesson extending a bit further down the slope, above Les Petits Charrons." [HIGH]
- **Above** Tessons on the slope: Les Luchets, Les Tillets, Les Vireuils, Les Narvaux (the high, steely band). Roulot's own comparison (Marsh 2024): Tessons is "a synthesis of Tillets and Meix-Chavaux," combining the finesse of the higher parcels with the body of the lower ones. Winehog: Roulot's Tessons combines "the minerality of the higher located village wines like Luchets and Tillets with a 1er cru like weight and persistence." [HIGH]
- **Style vs. neighbours.** Essa: "Vin de race et d'élégance... toujours très minéral avec un nez de silex" with "volume puissant mais élégant", developing lime-blossom (tilleul) with age. Jasper Morris (Inside Burgundy overview): "multi-layered" with "terrific density of flavour" and "occasional lime blossom notes." Rare Wine Co.: "quintessential Meursault from the heart of the appellation: powerful, long and complex." [HIGH on the consensus: Tessons = the most "complete"/powerful of Roulot's village wines, with flint and lime-blossom]
- Benjamin Lewin MW (2014 visit) reported that in the 2013 line-up "Tessons was more powerful" than Meix Chavaux, Tillets, Luchets and Narvaux, and quoted Jean-Marc on what separates the lieux-dits: exposure, elevation on the slope, and clay/limestone proportion — with a week's harvest difference between Luchets and Narvaux. [HIGH]

### Ownership map

- Essa (2019): nine proprietors in the ~5.3 ha. [HIGH on count]
- Producers bottling a Tessons (winehog 2017): **Roulot** (0.85 ha, clos), **Jean-Philippe Fichet**, **Pierre Morey**, **Michel Bouzereau**, **Buisson-Charles**, **Darviot-Perrin**, **Domaine Caillot**, **Morey-Blanc**, **Vincent Sauvestre**. [HIGH on names; individual hectarages NOT FOUND beyond Roulot's]
- Note for the table: Pierre Morey's Tessons and Fichet's Tesson are the two most often set against Roulot's. Roulot's is the only walled monopole-within-the-climat.

## 2. Roulot's plot: "Clos de Mon Plaisir" / "À Mon Plaisir, Clos du Haut Tesson"

- **Size:** 0.85 ha. [HIGH — Kermit Lynch tech sheet; Wikipedia holdings list; Grand Cru Selections]
- **Position within Tessons:** the upper part of the climat — the official label since the late 2010s reads "À Mon Plaisir, Clos du Haut Tesson" (i.e. the *upper* Tesson), so the clos is in the high sector of the lieu-dit, bordering the upper village climats. [HIGH on "Haut Tesson" from the label; MED that this is the cadastral sub-name]. Rare Wine Co: "lies north of the premier crus at mid-slope elevation." Grand Cru Selections: "situated north of the 1er Crus in the heart of the appellation."
- **Walls:** a true walled clos, "Roulot's clos planted during the 1950s" (Marsh). Guy Roulot built/assembled the domaine in the 1950s–60s "by purchasing, renting, and planting village parcels." Whether the wall pre-dates Guy is NOT FOUND. [MED]
- **Name change:** The wine was long labelled "Meursault Les Tessons, Clos de Mon Plaisir"; Inside Burgundy's overview and Wine-Searcher now list it as "Meursault, À Mon Plaisir, Clos du Haut Tesson" (Jasper: "the renamed..."). I could not find a sourced explanation for the change (INAO labelling rules for the word "Clos" and for lieu-dit names are the likely reason, but this is inference). [MED on the fact of renaming; reason NOT FOUND]
- **Vine age / planting:** 1951 and 1961 (Kermit Lynch tech sheet; Kermit Lynch 2013 product page). Wikipedia says 1961 only. So in 2011 the vines were 50–60 years old. [HIGH]
- **Acquired from whom / when:** NOT FOUND precisely. Marsh says Guy Roulot assembled the domaine in the 1950s–60s via purchase, rental and planting; Kermit Lynch attributes expansion to "his marriage to Geneviève Coche and strategic acquisitions." Whether Tessons came via the Coche side or by purchase is not stated. [LOW]
- **Rootstock / selection:** NOT FOUND for Tessons. (For context: Nanson 2016 notes Roulot's Tillets was on SO4 rootstock with declining yields and was grubbed up after 2015. The Fine Wine Experience 2018 write-up says Roulot "uses clones", contrasting with Coche's massal-only policy — [LOW], single retailer-event source.)
- **Farming:**
  - Herbicide-free / "organic in practice" from Jean-Marc's early years: Rare Wine Co. and Grand Cru Selections say organic since 1989/early 1990s; Jasper Morris says "transitioned to organic farming in 1999"; Jamie Goode (2017) and Vintry: "organic since 2000, certified since 2013." The domaine's own Kermit Lynch sheet simply says "farmed organically (certified)." Best reconstruction: herbicides abandoned ~1989–1990s, full organic from ~1999–2000, **Ecocert certification from the 2013 vintage**, with biodynamic experiments (Jasper; Wikipedia says biodynamic practices from 2012). [HIGH on certified 2013; MED on start date]
  - Jean-Marc to Marsh (2024) on going organic: "I had no choice."
  - Soil is ploughed; Guyot and cordon pruning; short/early pruning; de-budding for yield control; rigorous sorting at harvest. [HIGH — Kermit Lynch sheet]
  - Horse ploughing: NOT FOUND.

## 3. Winemaking for this cuvée (Roulot whites), and 2011 specifics

### Harvest
- 2011 harvest dates at Roulot: NOT FOUND precisely. Context: Nanson's autumn-2012 "Vintage Viewpoint" describes 2011 as "notably precocious — only the sixth time on record that harvesting commenced in August," with "uncommonly clean" fruit where sorting was mostly for unripe rather than rotten bunches. Roulot is an early picker by philosophy ("early harvest dates" is the first of Jean-Marc's three reasons his wines are pale — Lewin 2014), so an end-of-August/first-days-of-September start is likely but unsourced. [LOW on dates]
- Yield: 2011 is the domaine's internal *reference* (full-crop) year — Jean-Marc (Nanson, Oct 2016): "In 2016 everyone expected that they had lost 80%, but in the end it was 62% lost for us, versus our reference of 2011." The 2012 hail cost "-60%" against the same reference (oenologist Annabel Sieve, Nanson 2017). [HIGH]

### Pressing — the signature step
- Whole bunches are **lightly crushed (foulage) before pressing** so that juice can be extracted at extremely low pressure. Jean-Marc (Marsh 2024): 75% of the juice at 0.2 bar ("essentially free-run from skin breaking"), the next 15% at 0.9 bar, the final 10% at 1.6 bar. "If you don't crush, that last part would be 40 percent." He fully oxidises (hyper-oxidises) that last 10% to precipitate unstable phenolics, while retaining the "good" phenolics for ageing. To Jamie Goode (2017): "We get most of the juice out at low pressure" — 0.2 then 0.6 bar for 90% of yield, up to 1.6 bar for the rest; "It contributes a lot to the style." Jasper Morris: controlled crushing before pressing yields "a greener juice" with a better (lower) pH. [HIGH]
- Speed: "So if a plot is harvested on Monday, by Tuesday morning it is in barrel" (Goode 2017). [HIGH]

### Settling, lees, fermentation
- Overnight décantation only; deliberately turbid juice; the lees are homogenised across barrels via a secondary vat so every barrel has the same turbidity (Goode 2017). [HIGH]
- Indigenous yeasts; fermentation in barrel (Rare Wine Co.; Grand Cru Selections; Kermit Lynch). [HIGH]
- No sandstone jars for Tessons: Roulot now uses ~500 L sandstone (grès) vessels, concrete and three 1,200 L Stockinger foudres for some cuvées, but Marsh (2024) records that "Tessons receives no sandstone treatment." These vessels post-date 2011 in any case. [HIGH]

### Barrel regime
- Cooper: **Damy** (Meursault) — Goode 2017. [HIGH]
- New oak for the village lieux-dits: 15% (Goode 2017); "15–18%" domaine average (Kermit Lynch sheet); "20%" village (Wikipedia; Jasper: max 20% village, 25–30% 1er cru); "10–30% depending on wine and vintage" (Rare Wine Co.). Best figure for a village lieu-dit like Tessons in 2011: **~15–20% new**. [HIGH on the range]
- Forest / toast: NOT FOUND.

### Bâtonnage
- Light stirring every 2–3 (Goode) or 3–4 weeks (Kermit Lynch) *only until malolactic is complete*, then none. Rare Wine Co: "maximum 6 times, only in high-acidity years." Jean-Marc's quote: "It does, I admit, create richness, but it also creates heaviness, and one loses purity." [HIGH]

### Élevage length, racking, tank phase — the Roulot innovation
- ~11–12 months in barrel on lees (first racking postponed until just before the next harvest), then racked to **stainless steel for 6–7 months on fine lees**, bottling Feb–April of the second year (i.e. ~18 months). Kermit Lynch: "11 months... then racked to stainless steel for 7 more months." Nanson (2015): "Everything ages in barrel during the first year, then finishes in tank for 6 months before bottling in February–April." [HIGH]
- Why: Jean-Marc (Marsh 2024): "If you bottle at 11 months, you close on the fruit and you lose something." He extended élevage after 1988 and especially after 1993 (incomplete malo with residual sugar) and found "longer aging integrated the acidity very differently, and the texture was more silky." He said he was **influenced by tasting Dominique Lafon's wines**, first ran two barrel sets, then moved to stainless steel for the second winter. To Goode: "We get microoxygenation in the wood, then reduction in stainless steel. It gives the wine vertical tension." [HIGH]
- Note on the brief's assumption "early bottling of village lieux-dits": I found **no** evidence that Roulot bottles the village lieux-dits earlier than the 1er crus; every source describes one regime (12 + 6 months) for all whites except Aligoté. The *historical* contrast is that Guy Roulot's generation bottled at ~11–12 months and Jean-Marc lengthened it. [HIGH]

### Malolactic, SO2, fining/filtration, closures, premox
- Malo completes in barrel over the first winter/spring (Nanson 2015: Feb–April). [HIGH]
- SO2: "significantly reduced" (Jasper); since 2016 "lower doses at the start of fermentation" (Wikipedia). 2011-specific SO2 numbers: NOT FOUND. [MED]
- Fining/filtration (Goode 2017): "eight of ten times" fined with casein + bentonite, decided by blind comparison; 5-micron filtration before bottling. Jean-Marc: "Sometimes people are very reactive about filtering but there are hundreds of ways to filter." [HIGH]
- Closure for 2011: NOT FOUND (natural cork assumed; no DIAM statement found for Roulot in any fetched source). [LOW]
- **Premox record** (gdeschamps.net "Oxidized Burgundies" wiki, community-sourced): Roulot has a real but moderate history — 1996 Meix-Chavaux, 1999 Perrières "3 of 5 oxidised" (2008), Tessons oxidised bottles reported for 1999, 2001, 2002 ("badly oxidised", Sept 2010), 2005 Tillets, 2007 Tillets/Vireuils, 2008 Tessons (Oct 2015). For **2011**, the only entry is one Meix-Chavaux (June 2020); **no 2011 Tessons premox report** in that database. The 2024 Noble Rot vertical (Marsh) found the 1996 bottle cork-compromised but the 2004–2017 run sound. [HIGH on the database contents; it is community data]
- Reductive style: yes — the stainless phase is explicitly for reduction; Curtis (Decanter 2024) calls Roulot the pioneer of the "early picked, lively and slightly reductive" Meursault style; Berserker note on the 2011 Tessons (2020) opens with "cool flint, matchstick." [HIGH]

### What differed in 2011 at Roulot
- Nothing structural found. 2011 was the first vintage of the Clos des Bouchères monopole (from the January 2011 Manuel/Bohr deal, split with Lafon — see §5), so the 2011 range was the first with both a new 1er cru monopole and the full lieu-dit line-up; it was also the last "full" crop before the 2012 hail (-60%) and 2013 hail. [HIGH]

## 4. The 2011 vintage for this wine

### Season and general character
- Nanson (autumn 2012): precocious year, harvest commencing in August for the sixth time on record; clean fruit; pre-harvest rain swelled berries so sugars were modest; the whites "seem to have come around very nicely, particularly those harvested slightly earlier than average," with freshness better than 2006/2009 but without the sharp acidity of 2007/2008/2010. [HIGH]
- Jean-Marc Roulot (to Marsh, 2024): "There was no excess of rain or frost in 2011. I don't know why 2011 does not have a higher reputation. I prefer it to my 2010, which had botrytis. 2011 is a great vintage." [HIGH — verbatim]
- Winehog on Roulot's 2011 Luchets (Aug 2016): "Jean-Marc Roulot really like his 2011s … I can understand why." [HIGH]
- Linden Wilkie (Fine Wine Experience, July 2022, "2011 Burgundy – Reviewing the First Class Cabin"): 2011 is "a classic, well-balanced vintage" for whites, most "ready to go," a year whose conditions were "sufficiently challenging to separate the top tier estates from the less strict." [HIGH]

### Reviews of Roulot Tessons Clos de Mon Plaisir 2011
- **Stephen Tanzer / IWC-Vinous (Sept–Oct 2013), 91 (Thatcher's) or 92 (SommPicks — retailer transcription discrepancy):** "Bright, pale lemon-yellow. Higher-toned than the Tillets, offering scents of lemon and hazelnut. Ripe but tightly wound, with strong acidity and stony cut giving the intense citrus flavors an almost painful quality. This backward, vibrant wine is a bit hard-edged today and in need of at least a couple years of cellaring." [HIGH on text; MED on 91 vs 92 — Thatcher's 91 is the more careful listing]
- **Burghound (Allen Meadows): 92** (Thatcher's and SommPicks both list 92; text NOT FOUND). [MED]
- **Wine Advocate 94 / Jancis 92+ / Suckling 18/20** as listed by SommPicks for the 1.5 L — I could not verify these against the 2011 vintage specifically; SommPicks score boxes are often pulled from other vintages. Treat as [LOW] / unverified.
- **Sarah Marsh MW (WFW, Mar 2024, Noble Rot vertical):** "Like many, it has aged better than initially expected. The 2011s are lovely now, and this Tessons is pleasing, light-bodied, trim, and slimly textured. Just nicely fresh and silky." [HIGH]
- **Linden Wilkie (July 2022): 91** — "subdued nose" with "resin"; early-picked style "lean" next to peers; "low key, good, but doesn't sing." [HIGH]
- **WineBerserkers, 20 June 2020, 91:** "Light to medium gold color. Nose of cool flint, matchstick, hyacinth, taut grapefruit, apple core... Bright acidity and tensile structure. Long finish of grippy white peach, lemon, apple, and chalky rock... Pure and scintillating." A replying poster: Roulot "really overperformed on the 11s." [HIGH, community]
- **CellarTracker:** community average 91 (per Thatcher's listing); the wine page itself was blocked. [MED]
- Vintage-neighbour context from the same vertical (Marsh): 2010 "had botrytis" (Roulot); 2013 "slim-bodied... not a lot of stuffing"; 2009 "sumptuous but elegant and energetic"; 2007 and 2017 her top picks.

### Drinking window / bottle variation
- Consensus now: at peak plateau, "ready to go" (Wilkie 2022), "lovely now" (Marsh 2024); fresh bottles in 2020–2024 show flint/matchstick reduction and citrus — no oxidative drift reported for 2011 Tessons. Expect it to be light-bodied and trim rather than rich; serve it before the Coche, not after. [HIGH]

### Market
- Thatcher's: $420 (750 ml, 2011). SommPicks 1.5 L sold out. Wine-Searcher page blocked. Verve lists the 2023 at $399, so 2011 back-vintage trades at or slightly above current release. [MED]

## 5. Stories, people, connections (Roulot)

### The family and Guy Roulot
- Roulots in Meursault since 1798; domaine "founded 1830 by Guillaume Roulot" (Wikipedia; Grand Cru Selections says 1820; Verve says 1930 — retail error). Six generations. [HIGH on 19th-century origin; MED on exact year]
- The family were **distillers** first — marc and fine de Bourgogne — and only under Guy Roulot (1929–1982) shifted to vinifying and bottling separately, "a novelty at the time" (Kermit Lynch). Guy built the modern domaine from the 1950s by buying, renting and planting, and married **Geneviève Coche, granddaughter of Léon Coche** — founder of what became Coche-Dury. [HIGH]
- **Guy's big idea**: bottling village lieux-dits separately instead of one village blend — Marsh: "among the first producers to make separate bottlings of village lieux-dits." Jean-Marc (Lewin 2014): "The style was defined in the sixties by the decisions my father made to separate the cuvées." (Kelley credits René Lafon's Clos de la Barre as the other pioneer.) [HIGH]
- **Judgment of Paris 1976**: Roulot's Meursault-Charmes 1973 placed 2nd in the white flight, 126.5 points to Montelena's 132 (Wikipedia); a recent re-analysis argues the two tied on the nine-judge panel. Good dinner-table fact. [HIGH]
- Guy died in 1982 aged 53. [HIGH]

### The interregnum 1982–1988: Ted Lemon and Franck Grux (not Lafon)
- **Ted Lemon**, American, Dijon oenology degree 1981, hired 1982–84 on the recommendation of Jacques Seysses (Dujac) — "the first American hired as winemaker and vineyard manager of a Burgundian estate"; Burgundians called him "Le Comte de Citron"; later founded Littorai (1993). [HIGH on Lemon's tenure — Vintry, Marsh, Jasper, Grand Cru Selections; MED on the Seysses detail, single secondary source]
- **Franck Grux**, Jean-Marc's cousin, ran the cellar 1984–1988, then was recruited by Olivier Leflaive, where he became the long-time winemaker. [HIGH]
- Jean-Marc returned for the **1988 élevage / October 1988** (Marsh; Vintry) and is listed as running the domaine from 1989. [HIGH]
- **Dominique Lafon**: Jasper Morris's 40-year Lafon retrospective shows Dominique in 1982 at Weinbach, 1983–86 as Becky Wasserman's right-hand man, full-time at Comtes Lafon from 1986 — *no* stint at Roulot. Lafon's relationship to Roulot is (i) stylistic: Jean-Marc credits tasting Lafon's wines for his move to longer élevage; (ii) commercial: the 2011 partition of Domaine René Manuel; (iii) Meursault-neighbour friendship. The "Lafon interregnum" claim is unsupported — NOT FOUND. [HIGH]

### Jean-Marc Roulot, actor
- Trained and worked as a stage/film actor in Paris; kept acting after taking over the domaine. Films cited: Cédric Klapisch's *Ce qui nous lie / Back to Burgundy* (2017), playing a vigneron; Trần Anh Hùng's *La Passion de Dodin Bouffant / The Taste of Things* (2023, with Juliette Binoche), where he plays the forester who demonstrates the ortolan. Marsh: he is "rather solemn when discussing his wine" but lights up talking about acting. [HIGH]
- Age: 61 in June 2017 (Goode) → born c. 1955–56. [MED]
- Levi Dalton, *I'll Drink to That* episode 55, 8 Jan 2013, 1h12 — content not transcribed in any fetched source. [HIGH on existence]

### Philosophy — quotable lines
- "Excess is the enemy of terroir – too much alcohol or too much oak." (Lewin 2014)
- "Our wine is lightly colored for three reasons: early harvest dates, not too much oak, and not too much bâtonnage." (Lewin 2014)
- "If you see the difference in the glass it's justified, but if you don't there is no point." — on splitting cuvées (Lewin 2014)
- "The window (for picking) is very, very narrow in early September because the days are longer, while at the end of September they are colder and shorter." (Marsh 2024)
- "It is a shame so many people come to Burgundy in November... The difference between then and January to March is huge." (Goode 2017)
- "Charmes is earth, Genevrières is the air, Perrières is the fire." (Nanson 2021)
- Marc de Bourgogne: in 2018 Jean-Marc distilled the pomace of five climats separately, honouring the family's distilling past; on Tessons marc: "The brandy is like the wine. Tessons has a bit more flesh than Luchets." (Marsh 2024) [HIGH]

### Holdings snapshot (for context at table)
- ~12.7 ha white / 0.83 ha red (Jasper); ~15–16 ha including 2011 Manuel lease (Wikipedia/Goode). 1er crus: Perrières 0.26 ha (planted 1964, bought 1976), Clos des Bouchères 1.38 ha monopole (from 2011), Porusot 0.42, Charmes 0.28 (planted 1942, "70-year-old vines"), Monthelie Champs Fulliots 0.19. # WINE (b): DOMAINE COCHE-DURY — MEURSAULT "LES ROUGEOTS" 2011

## Headline talking points (30 seconds)

- **Rougeots is NOT near the Puligny border.** It is on the north side of Meursault, "just north of Les Tesson extending a bit further down the slope, above Les Petits Charrons" (winehog), mid-slope, east-facing, directly overlooking the old village. The Puligny association belongs to Coche's *Enseignères* parcel, not Rougeots. [HIGH]
- The name comes from the soil: iron-oxide-stained, reddish-brown earth ("la terre chargée d'oxyde de fer se pare en superficie de reflets bruns rouges" — Essa). [HIGH]
- Coche's parcel: 0.65–0.73 ha planted 1940, 1962, 1973; vines therefore 38–71 years old in 2011; all massal selection — the domaine plants "no clones of any kind." [HIGH]
- 2011 was the **first vintage after Raphaël Coche formally took over (2010)**, though he had been at the domaine since 1997/1999 and Jean-François remained present; Neal Martin (WA, 2014): oak "just loitering in the background"; William Kelley (WA, Dec 2018), 94: "an excellent but comparatively precocious vintage for Coche-Dury." [HIGH]
- The Coche method: firm crushing, long slow pressing on the old Vaslin (pneumatic press added 2009), lots of lees, barrel fermentation and ~15–22 months in barrel including a second winter, historically ~50% new oak (now ~20–25%), hand-bottled by *chèvre à deux becs*, unfined/unfiltered whites. The "struck match / gunpowder / popcorn" signature is a lees-and-reduction product, and it is present in the 2011 Rougeots per multiple notes. [HIGH]

## 1. The vineyard / climat: Les Rougeots (Le Rougeot)

### Location, exposure, altitude, size

- **Position.** Winehog (Öhman, 2017): "Meursault Les Rougeot is located just north of Les Tesson extending a bit further down the slope, above Les Petits Charrons." Essa (2019): Rougeots occupies "a triangular sector midway up an east-facing slope, positioned between Chevalières and Tessons" and "directly overlooks the historic village." Guide Hachette: "born in mid-slope and fairly central." So: north side of the village, mid-slope, below/north of Tessons, above Petits-Charrons, adjoining Chevalières — the cluster of five that Öhman calls the elite northern village climats. [HIGH]
- **On the brief's question ("low-slope, near Puligny border?")**: No. Rougeots is north of the village, i.e. on the Volnay/Monthelie side, well away from Puligny. It is mid-slope, not low-slope; the lower, richer village band near the Ruisseau des Cloux and the plain is a different sector (Curtis). The Puligny connection is Coche's *Puligny-Montrachet Les Enseignères* (bought 1985), which sits below Bâtard on the Meursault/Puligny line. [HIGH]
- **Exposure:** east. [HIGH]
- **Altitude:** not given numerically anywhere I could reach; "almost the same altitude as the 1er crus south of the village" (winehog) and slightly below Tessons (~260 m), so roughly 245–260 m. [MED, inferred]
- **Size:** Lavalle 1855: "Le Rougeot (3.1740 ha)." Modern cadastral figure NOT FOUND; Essa says the climat has three distinct sections, one of which is a genuine walled *clos* (former Sarraut property), now bottled occasionally by négociants Verget and Deux Montille as "Clos Rougeot" (Latimer Vintners lists a 2022 Lafouge "Clos Rougeot"). [HIGH on Lavalle; MED on modern sub-division]

### Soil / geology and etymology

- Essa: "Au milieu d'un coteau orienté à l'Est, la terre chargée d'oxyde de fer se pare en superficie de reflets bruns rouges" — iron-oxide-rich soil with reddish-brown surface tints; hence the name. Essa explicitly rejects the alternative folk-etymology that links the name to *rougeot*, a vine disease (red leaf/"rougeau"), in favour of the soil colour. [HIGH]
- Same broad geology as Tessons (argilo-calcaire with iron), but Rougeots is described as warmer and more precocious. [HIGH]

### Classification history

- Lavalle 1855: *Deuxième Cuvée* (Rougeots 3.17 ha alongside Grands-Charrons 15.05 ha and Chevalières 10.39 ha) — one rung below Tessons' *Première Cuvée*. [HIGH]
- 1862: 2e classe with Chevalières, Tesson, Charrons. [HIGH]
- Rodier 1920: *Deuxième Cuvée* with Grands-Charrons, Petits-Charrons, Chevalières, Tesson. [HIGH]
- AOC: village. No 1er cru bid found. Hedonism's listing calling it "the Premier Cru climat Les Rougeots" is a retailer error. [HIGH]

### Neighbours and how Rougeots differs

- Above/south: Tessons. Adjacent: Chevalières (Coche also bottles a Chevalières — Kelley characterises Chevalières as "piercing tension" and Rougeots as "greater amplitude and authority"). Below: Petits-Charrons / Grands-Charrons. [HIGH]
- Essa: Rougeots is "l'un des plus complets de la commune," combining the qualities of neighbouring Tessons while expressing "warmth and precocity"; he rates its walled section as comparable in quality to Clos des Perrières. [HIGH]
- Jasper Morris on Coche's Rougeots: shows "green tints to the fruit" when young, eventually a "generous palate which retains precision," with excellent ageing potential. [HIGH]

### Ownership map

- Winehog calls Rougeots "Coche-Dury territory." Other bottlers found: **Ropiteau Frères** (Boisset; Hachette 2006 listing, 600 bottles), **Verget** and **Deux Montille** (the Clos Rougeot — Essa), **Domaine Lafouge** ("Clos Rougeot" 2022, Latimer), **Domaine Rougeot** (the family, not necessarily the climat — coincidence of names, [LOW]). Hectarages for other owners NOT FOUND. [MED]

## 3. Winemaking for Coche's whites, and what 2011 means

### The transition — who made the 2011?
- Timeline: Raphaël at the domaine from 1997 (Curtis) or 1999 full-time after viticulture/oenology studies (Wikipedia, Nanson 2007); "assumed winemaking responsibilities" 2010 (Wikipedia); "Jean-François retired; Raphaël assumed full control" 2010 (Curtis; Jasper); domaine now run by Raphaël and wife Charline. [HIGH]
- Meaning for 2011: the 2011s are the second vintage under Raphaël's formal direction (2010 the first), made with Jean-François still at hand — Thatcher's copy for the 2011 Perrières says "Raphaël and Jean-François produced" it. Raphaël: "My father and I see eye-to-eye on winemaking" (Curtis). The style shifts attributed to Raphaël (less new oak, fewer lees, less trituration, pneumatic press) were gradual and began in 2009 with the pneumatic press. Kelley (WA 2018) on the 2011s: "shaping up to be an excellent but comparatively precocious vintage for Coche-Dury." [HIGH]
- Labels still read "Domaine J.-F. Coche-Dury" in 2011 (winehog, K&L listing). [HIGH]

### Harvest
- 2011 harvest dates at Coche: NOT FOUND. Coche's philosophy is early picking to keep "nerve"; whites are deliberately "never among the ripest or highest in alcohol" (Kermit Lynch). [MED]
- Yields: 2011 is the first of what Raphaël called the "short vintages (2011–2014)" that pushed him into small négociant purchases for security (Jasper 2021) — note the contrast with Roulot, for whom 2011 was a *full* reference crop. Coche's low yields are structural (old vines, short pruning), so "short" here is relative. [HIGH on the quote; MED on interpretation]

### Crushing and pressing — the Coche signature
- Grapes are **crushed before pressing** — Jasper: "firmly crushed before pressing"; Curtis: "lightly crushed"; Curtis also records whole-bunch pressing in Decanter 2024 ("emphasizes whole-bunch pressing"). Reconcile: bunches go whole into the press but are crushed/trodden (foulage/trituration) in it; Raphaël has *reduced* trituration since the pneumatic press (Jasper 2021: "fewer lees and reduced trituration (skin mashing) since adopting the pneumatic press"). [HIGH that foulage + long pressing with abundant lees is the historical method]
- Presses: traditional **Vaslin** horizontal (Raphaël: the Vaslin is "a Formula One version" — Jasper 2021) plus a **pneumatic press since 2009**; Curtis: the pneumatic added "to obtain discrete, elegantly bitter notes from the phenolics." The Berserkers thread's line — the family has "a rare aptitude... for knowing how hard to press and for how long" — captures the lore. In 2011, both presses were in use. [HIGH]

### Fermentation, lees, oak
- Barrel fermentation, indigenous yeast (Kermit Lynch; bestofwines). [HIGH]
- Lees: abundant; "extended lees contact prevents oxidation" (Kermit Lynch sheet). Bâtonnage: yes, "regular" (bestofwines; Fine Wine Experience table). Raphaël now uses "less new oak, less bâtonnage and less lees" than his father (Berserkers summary, [MED]). [HIGH on bâtonnage as a Coche practice; MED on the reduction under Raphaël]
- **New oak:** Nanson (2007): "approximately 50% new oak"; Kermit Lynch sheet: "larger-than-usual doses of new wood"; Curtis (2022): "reduced to 20–25% new wood for most wines (down from Jean-François era)"; Jasper: "modest new wood (≤25%)"; winewithseth: village modest, up to 50% for Corton-Charlemagne. For **Rougeots 2011**: most plausibly 25–35% new — village-level share of a regime that was moving from ~50% toward ~25%. No cuvée-specific 2011 figure found. [MED]
- Cooper: Damy (Meursault); the domaine reportedly selects and dries its own wood before Damy makes the barrels (winewithseth, [MED — single secondary source]). [MED]

### Élevage, racking, bottling
- Long: "15 to 22 months in barrel depending on vintage" (Kermit Lynch); "an average 22 months" (Nanson 2007); Curtis: "15 months to nearly 2 years"; Jasper: racked in July, blended, back to cask for a second winter, bottled in April of the second year. [HIGH]
- Slow malo, long fermentation (Curtis). [HIGH]
- Unfined, unfiltered whites (Kermit Lynch; winewithseth); reds egg-white fined. [HIGH]
- Bottling: hand-bottled by *chèvre à deux becs*, maximum eight casks a day (Curtis); a bottling line has since been installed for some cuvées (Jasper 2021; Curtis). In 2011: hand-bottled. [HIGH]
- Raphaël's philosophy: "There is no recipe – the élevage depends upon the life the vine has lived during the season"; the wine "must be manipulated as little as possible" — no pushing, aerating, or thermal shock (Curtis). [HIGH]
- SO2: "minimal" (bestofwines) — numbers NOT FOUND. Closures: natural cork; no DIAM statement found. [LOW]

### Reduction and premox
- The signature "struck match / gunpowder / popcorn / hazelnut" reduction is the house marker (winehog: "classic Coche notes – popcorn and gun powder"; Jancis on the 2011 Rougeots, Dec 2024: "Strong struck-match..."; Berserkers 2023: "Textbook matchstick Coche aromatics"). The Berserkers Coche thread claims the most *pronounced* reduction years were 1999 and 2007 and that Raphaël has dialled it back ("I want to taste the terroir"), but the 2011 notes show it is still there. [HIGH]
- Premox database (gdeschamps.net): Coche has a lighter record than most Meursault houses — 1992 Corton-Charlemagne (all nine bottles oxidised, one collector), 1996 Bourgogne/Caillerets, 1999 Rougeots one bottle "partially oxidized" (2011), 2007 Rougeots "on the fast highway to premoxville" (2011). **2011: no oxidation issues reported.** [HIGH on database; community data]
- Winehog's 1992 Rougeots (Jan 2016) was "slightly maderized" — age, not premox. [MED]

## 5. Stories, people, connections (Coche-Dury)

### Lineage
- **Léon Coche** founded the domaine in the early 1920s (Curtis: 1923) as Coche-Bouillicaut, buying in Meursault, Auxey-Duresses and Monthélie; mixed bulk/bottle sales. Léon's descendants split: his son **Georges** took his share in 1964 and moved to estate bottling; Léon's granddaughter **Geneviève** married Guy Roulot. [HIGH]
- **Jean-François Coche** (b. c. 1950s; began in the vines at 14 with Georges) took over in 1973; married **Odile Dury** in 1975 — the Dury vineyards and the double-barrelled name date from then. Under Jean-François the domaine became, in Jasper Morris's words, "arguably the most sought-after internationally of all white burgundy domaines." Parker: "one of the greatest winemakers on planet earth" / "one of the five or six best white-wine makers in Burgundy"; Coates: "one of the finest white wine domaines in the world"; Jancis: "certainly the most reliable source of great white Burgundies." [HIGH]
- 1985: métayage for 0.33 ha Corton-Charlemagne (first vintage 1986); 1998 a helicopter crash destroyed ten rows of it; 2012 expanded to 0.88 ha (half owned, half leased). [HIGH]
- **Raphaël Coche** (worked alongside his father from age 15; at the domaine 1997/1999; in charge 2010) with wife **Charline**. Négoce: after the short 2011–14 crops and the 2016 frost, he bought Mâconnais grapes picked by his own team with Olivier Merlin's guidance, and continues to buy small lots of Bourgogne Blanc/Meursault from neighbours plus select Hospices de Beaune cuvées (Jasper 2021). [HIGH]
- Georges Coche's reputation came from local competition success; the international fame came with Parker in the late 1980s/early 1990s (Nanson 2007). Nanson's 2007 profile is notable for *dissenting* — questioning "universal excellence" and the middleman mark-ups relative to sane ex-cellar prices. [HIGH]

### The mystique and allocation
- Kermit Lynch's anecdote (importer sheet): when Lynch asked when they could meet, Jean-François replied "Only in the evening when I come back from the vines." [HIGH]
- Distribution: ~70% estate-bottled; ~30% of production sold to négociants (Louis Latour, Louis Jadot) under long contracts (Wikipedia; winewithseth). Different cuvées reach different markets: Kermit Lynch's US "Meursault" allocation has typically been the Narvaux bottling, while domaine-direct AC Meursault may come from Vireuils-Dessus, Narvaux or a blend (Berserkers thread, [MED]). [HIGH]
