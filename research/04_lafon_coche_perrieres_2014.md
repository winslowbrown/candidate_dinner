# Meursault 1er Cru Les Perrières 2014 — Domaine des Comtes Lafon & Domaine Coche-Dury

*Dossier for the Claud dinner, 26 Oct 2026. Companion to the Lafon Goutte d'Or 2014 and Coche-Dury Rougeots 2011 files, which carry the full domaine biographies; this file concentrates on the Perrières climat, the two parcels, and the two 2014 bottles.*

Confidence key: [HIGH] = stated by a primary source (domaine, importer) or by two independent expert sources; [MED] = single expert/critic source, or sources mildly disagree; [LOW] = retailer/community/secondary source only, or inference.

## PART A — THE CLIMAT: MEURSAULT 1ER CRU LES PERRIÈRES

### A1. Location, size, exposure, altitude

- **Position.** Southernmost of Meursault's great central band of premiers crus, on the Puligny side of the commune. It is "the only one of these three 1ers [Perrières, Charmes, Genevrières] that sits entirely above the road to Puligny" (Bill Nanson, burgundy-report.com, 2015, updated 2022) [HIGH]. Charmes (c. 31 ha) lies directly below it across the road; Genevrières (c. 16 ha) is its neighbour to the north; to the south "everything south of Meursault-Perrières is indeed in Puligny-Montrachet" — chiefly Puligny 1er Champ Canet, with Chalumaux and Blagny higher up (Nanson) [HIGH]. Stephen Tanzer describes Lafon's main parcel as being "at the northern end of the appellation, adjacent to Genevrières-Dessus" (Vinous, Dec 2019) [HIGH].
- **Size.** 13 ha 21 a 17 ca (Patrick Essa, vigneron at Buisson-Charles, degustateurs.pro, citing the cadastre) [HIGH]; Nanson: "a little over 13 hectares" with **27 owners** on Laurent Gotti's 2015 producer map [HIGH]; Nanson's 2019 Grivault article gives 13.21 ha total [HIGH]. (Older secondary sources say 13.71 ha — Jockovino 2010 [LOW].)
- **Sub-lieux-dits (cadastral, per Essa):** Aux Perrières 0 ha 79 a 90 ca; Les Perrières Dessus 3 ha 76 a 29 ca; Les Perrières Dessous 9 ha 59 a 50 ca (of which the Clos des Perrières = 95 a, leaving 8 ha 64 a 98 ca of "open" Dessous) [HIGH].
- **Exposure/altitude.** "Perfect east–southeast exposure" (Jasper Morris MW, Sotheby's Epicurean's Atlas, 2023) [HIGH]. Lafon's parcels: "East/southeast facing with slopes between 10-16%" (Becky Wasserman & Co. tech sheet) [HIGH]. The Clos des Perrières sits at 260 m (Pascal Ribéreau-Gayon, quoted by Sommeliers International) [MED]. Winewithseth gives 280–310 m for the climat as a whole [LOW]. Essa notes Dessous lies roughly twenty metres below Dessus, separated by the old quarry [HIGH].

### A2. Name etymology — the quarry

- The name is not "little stones." Jasper Morris: "the derivation of Perrières is not 'little stones' but a reference to a former quarry here, dating back centuries to a time when limestone was needed to build the houses in the village" (Sotheby's, 2023) [HIGH]. iDealwine derives it from Latin *petrarium* (stone quarry) [MED].
- Nanson: "Perrières is not an uncommon name in Burgundy … all take their names from the ancient extraction of stone to build the local villages – quarries. Among other places and houses, the quarried stone from here was used for the bell-tower of the church in the centre of Meursault" [HIGH].
- Essa: the higher block (Dessus + Aux Perrières) "surplombe des carrières qui ont fortement entaillé le coteau" — the quarries cut deeply into the slope; what was extracted was "le marbre de Bourgogne ou pierre de Chassagne," used for the walls and houses of the bourg. He adds that over time "le Cru a ainsi perdu deux hectares disparus en poussière" — roughly two hectares of the climat were literally quarried away into dust [HIGH, single but expert local source].
- The quarry is still visible in the appellation map: right in the middle of the vineyard there are parcels (Château de Meursault, de Montille) "actually only rated as 'Bourgogne' or 'Villages Meursault' – due to being a quarry hole that was back-filled with soil and rubble" (Nanson) [HIGH]. Essa confirms: the partially filled quarry "porte encore des Vignes d'appellation « Bourgogne blanc »," and it "separates clearly the Dessus from the Dessous" [HIGH]. William Kelley, quoted on Domaine Pierre Morey's site: "the composition of the soils suggests that this was the location where rejected stones from the quarry that lends the climat its name were discarded" [MED]. Anne Morey: it is essentially "a heap of stones with vines growing straight out of the stones" [MED].
- Jockovino's claim that "no stone was actually extracted there" is contradicted by every local source and should be disregarded [HIGH].

### A3. Soils and geology

- Nanson (2015): "Geologically, the base stone is quite homogenous on a Chassagne stone lower down – a hard oolite limestone. There's more argilles and clay higher up with some Comblanchien limestone. Often there's no more than 20-30 centimetres of soil before the mother-rock." The upper sections are "a mix of the very stony and sometimes a little more sandy soil. Towards the top, under the trees there is often some degeneration of the vines – virus issues – mainly court-noué" [HIGH].
- Lafon's own description (via Grand Cru Selections/Kellogg importer sheets): "topsoil quite fine and white due to the high amount of limestone and marl, subsoils are flaked limestone" [HIGH]. Becky Wasserman: "Shallow white limestone and marl soil on crumbly limestone subsoil" [HIGH]. Tanzer: Lafon's main parcel is on "mostly whitish marl soil," the steep 1982 parcel on "slightly darker soil," the ex-Gauffroy parcel on "more reddish soil" [HIGH].
- Clos des Perrières: "Bathonian limestone at higher elevations; stony, red colluvium soil below" (Sommeliers International, on Grivault) [MED].
- Louis Latour: "a soil that is thinner and stonier than many of its immediate neighbours" [HIGH].
- Essa's sector-by-sector reading (the single most detailed published description) [HIGH as a vigneron's testimony, but interpretive]:
  - **"Grande Perrière"** — in Dessous, running on from Genevrières-Dessus "jusqu'à la grotte … de la 'Porre et Piarde'"; gives "the most civilised wines of the climat," closest to the great upper Genevrières facing it: "plus opulents, plus sensuels et plus immédiatement accessibles que le reste du Cru." Fairly steep; "terres blondes et caillouteuses" with good water retention and frost protection; rows run east–west; parcels from 25 ares to over 2 ha. **This is where Lafon's main 1955 parcel sits** (Tanzer places it at the northern end adjacent to Genevrières-Dessus).
  - **"Perrières Dessus"** — more fragmented, high and low parcels with variable orientation; gives a "côté rocailleux" that thickens the grain of the texture and "finit par imprimer une sensation quasi coupante sur la langue. Vin de cailloux sur des terres maigres" — evoking upper Chevalier or the rigidity of Goutte d'Or, but denser. Many walls and terraces "gagnées sur le calcaire dur à force de ténacité … Vins de pierres, mais sans doute aussi vin de sang et de labeur." **This is where Coche-Dury's vines are.**
  - **"Plat des Perrières"** — in Dessous, just above the "grande Charmes" (Charmes-Dessus), with the Clos as its epicentre, extending right to the Grande Perrière and left as a tongue that butts against Puligny Champ Canet. Nearly flat, slightly inclined east, stony. Essa: the Perrières from this sector "sont sans conteste les plus grands Bourgogne blancs par leur intensité," at Montrachet's level of complexity "mais sur un registre moins visqueux, plus salin et iodé." **Lafon's steep 2-ouvrée 1982 parcel is just south of the Clos and just above his Charmes-Dessus** (Tanzer).
- Character consensus: Nanson — "Good Perrières is often described as having a more linear, direct and mineral intensity – like the wines of Puligny"; "Perrières is a relatively early ripening terroir, similar to Charmes below" [HIGH]. Jasper Morris: "Perrières is certainly the weightiest of the Meursault vineyards and it should be the longest-lived … all the minerality of Genevrières, while the rounded appeal of Charmes shows here with greater power" [HIGH]. Charles Curtis MW (Decanter 2022): "Perrières is a warm terroir that ripens early" [HIGH]. Essa: "vin tellurique, énergique, violent, à la sauvagerie quasi dérangeante, qui déroute souvent lorsqu'il est jeune" [HIGH].

### A4. Perrières-Dessus vs Perrières-Dessous vs Clos des Perrières — which is best?

- Nanson: unlike Charmes and Genevrières (where the upper sections are better regarded), "in Perrières this is not the case." Producers "agree [Perrières-Dessous] to be the best part of this cru"; "amongst others, Coche-Dury and Bouchard Père might have another view, as most of their vines lie in the higher Perrières-Dessus section!" [HIGH].
- Essa (above) also ranks the flat Dessous/Clos sector highest, then the Grande Perrière, with Dessus giving the most "cutting," stony wines [HIGH].
- Yet Nanson's own tasting conclusion is that the Perrières-ness of the wines transcends the tiers: "these differences seem no more marked in the different tiers versus 50 metres apart in the same sections" [HIGH]. And the market's verdict is unambiguous: the most expensive Perrières in the world (Coche-Dury) comes from Dessus.
- **Clos des Perrières.** 94 a 52 ca (Grivault, via Sommeliers International; Nanson says 0.95 ha; Essa "un peu moins d'un hectare") [HIGH]. "A square Clos of vines that follows the road for 100 metres or-so … perfectly encompassed by a wall and sitting next to the road" (Nanson) [HIGH]. Created "by the Marquis de la Roche" at the beginning of the 19th century (Essa) — the Grivault website spells it "Marquis de La Troche"; Nanson writes "Marquess de la Roche who died with no heirs in 1879" [MED on the name; the family and date are consistent]. Albert Grivault (b. Meursault 1856, a distiller with a second distillery in Béziers) bought it from the heirs in 1879, aged 23 (Grivault website; Nanson) [HIGH]. Essa: buying "ce pré-carré en 1879 est assurément un coup de génie"; Grivault also created the Clos du Murger by his house and bought Clos Blanc in Pommard (planted in red) and Clos de Vougeot. In 1904 Albert Grivault bequeathed his Meursault Charmes to the Hospices de Beaune — the Cuvée Albert Grivault still sold every November (Grivault website) [HIGH]. The Clos is a monopole, now run by the fourth generation (Claire Bardet, manager; Henri-Marc Bardet, vinification; Michel Bardet still involved) [HIGH]. The domaine holds 1.5 ha of Perrières in total and is "the largest owner of the iconic Meursault 1er cru 'Perrières'" (Nanson 2019) [HIGH]. The 1893 Clos won a gold medal at the 1895 Paris show and was served at President Félix Faure's table (Sommeliers International) [MED].
- Nanson on the Clos: "excellent, and indeed a little different as it usually shows a more floral aromatic (heat of the Clos?) but no-one except the Bardets would say that it is the best" [HIGH].

### A5. The grand-cru-that-never-was

- **19th-century standing.** Jasper Morris: "Dr Lavalle, the expert 19th-century author of the first book about Burgundy to make a concerted effort to classify the vineyards, picked out Perrières as being the best white wine vineyard after the Montrachets, classing it as a rare tête de cuvée" (Sotheby's 2023) [HIGH]. Doctorwine: "From early nineteenth-century maps onward, it held the designation 'tête de cuvée, the highest quality accolade of the time'" [MED]. Essa: "sa réputation ancestrale l'a toujours positionné comme second après le 'Vrai Montrachet'" [HIGH]. Grivault's site cites Lavalle (1855) and Danguy & Aubertin (1892) as praising the Clos [HIGH]. The intermediate classifications (Beaune Comité d'agriculture 1860, Camille Rodier 1920) are known to have followed Lavalle's hierarchy with adjustments (monocepage.com) — I could NOT retrieve the specific Perrières line in Rodier; treat "tête de cuvée in Lavalle, top rank retained by Rodier" as [MED].
- **The 1936–37 decision.** Meursault "was not blessed with any Grands Crus when those jewels were handed out in the 1930s" (Jasper Morris) [HIGH]. The most specific published explanation comes from Patrick Essa (a Meursault vigneron): at the time of the AOC classification in the 1930s, "les décideurs des syndicats communaux … n'ont pas souhaité lui retirer la mention 'Meursault' en des temps où le consacrer grand Cru revenait à le nommer: « Les Perrières »." In other words, a grand cru would have been labelled simply "Les Perrières" (as Montrachet, Corton-Charlemagne etc. carry no village name), and the local syndicate preferred to keep the commercially valuable word Meursault on the label. He adds: "On sait aussi que des groupes de producteurs d'obédiences différentes se sont farouchement querellés pour générer ces décisions" — rival producer factions fought bitterly over it [HIGH as testimony; the archival paper trail is not online]. Raphaël Coche gives a compatible version: "when the time came to codify the appellations, the committees in each village were different — an explanation for the fact that there are no grands crus in Meursault," summarised in his line **"Tout est sous-classé à Meursault"** (Decanter, Charles Curtis MW, 2022) [HIGH].
- Essa's critique of the outcome: the general classification "le 'minore' et fait fi de son incomparable caractère de vin de pierres, taillé pour la très grande garde," leaving it "assez loin de son vrai rang de meilleur grand Cru après le Montrachet." He also raises the sceptic's question: with yields now roughly 2.5× the 15 hl/ha of the 1850s and planting density halved, does modern Perrières still dominate as it once did? [HIGH]
- **The Grivault dossier.** Nanson: "For many years, the Bardet family of Clos des Perrières have built a dossier with the aim of proposing their 'Clos des Perrières' for grand cru status." Other producers "will tell you that an AOC change only for the Clos des Perrières would always be opposed by other owners in the lower Perrières-Dessous section … but that if that whole lower section was considered, there might be some consensus" — while Coche-Dury and Bouchard, mostly in Dessus, "might have another view" [HIGH]. Winewithseth claims lobbying "stalled at INAO since early 2000s" [LOW]. Jasper Morris's own position: "There might indeed be a case for now promoting Les Perrières, but better perhaps to remain as the outstanding Premier Cru in Meursault." He adds the grand cru-style argument that Perrières "has the additional merit … of being good every year, not just in the great vintages" [HIGH].
- Useful dinner line: Nanson — "I don't find the weight of the grand crus of Puligny and Chassagne to the south, but weight alone is not really interesting, Perrières has energy and intensity – as good as any grand cru – and that's very interesting!"

## PART B — DOMAINE DES COMTES LAFON, MEURSAULT 1ER CRU PERRIÈRES 2014

### B1. Lafon's plot within Perrières

Best single source: Stephen Tanzer's Dec 2019 Vinous vertical (1984–2014), tasted with Dominique Lafon [HIGH throughout unless noted].

- **Total: 22 ouvrées ≈ 0.91–0.95 ha** ("nearly one hectare"; JM overview says 0.91 ha; Nanson's 2015 note "Own 1 hectare"; Becky Wasserman lists 0.77 ha for the main block).
- **Parcel 1 — 16 ouvrées (≈0.68 ha), Perrières-Dessous.** "Lies on mostly whitish marl soil at the northern end of the appellation, adjacent to Genevrières-Dessus; most of these vines are a sélection massale planted in 1955." This corresponds to Essa's "Grande Perrière" sector. "Following the 2015 harvest, Lafon pulled out about a quarter of these vines for replanting" — so the 2014 is the last vintage made from the intact 1955 massal block. Importer sheets confirm "planted in 1955 (87%) and 1983 (13%)" for the main 0.77 ha (Grand Cru Selections) and "2010, 1983, 1955 and earlier … massal and clonal selections" (Becky Wasserman).
- **Parcel 2 — 2 ouvrées (≈0.085 ha), Perrières-Dessous.** "A very steep 2 ouvrées … on slightly darker soil to the south of the Clos des Perrières, planted in 1982 just above his large, ideally situated parcel in Charmes-Dessus." (Importers round this to 1983.)
- **Parcel 3 — 4 ouvrées (≈0.17 ha), "Perrières Ginette."** Bought in 2004 from Ginette Gauffroy (Domaine Gauffroy) together with bits of Charmes and Meursault Crotots; "on more reddish soil next to Coche-Dury's Perrières, in the lowest part of Perrières-Dessus, just above and slightly to the south of his 2-ouvrée parcel; these vines are now more than 50 years old" (i.e., planted c. 1960s or earlier). The purchase "began to come into production in '09, following the expiration of pre-existing leases." Importers list it as 0.14 ha "Perrières Ginette."
- **"Among the oldest vines"?** Average vine age c. 55 years at the 2019 tasting (Tanzer): "made from mostly very old vines with an average age of around 55 years. Widespread court-noué (fanleaf virus) has steadily reduced the vigor of the vines." So in 2014 the 1955 block was 59 years old — old, though not uniquely so in this climat (Roulot's are 1950s; Coche's oldest are 1950) [HIGH]. Claims that Lafon's are "the oldest in Perrières" are NOT FOUND in any source.
- **Yields.** "Lafon's Perrières vines produced 50 hectoliters per hectare in 2009 but yields have never again reached that level; in fact, most recent years have been in the very low 20 to 30 hectoliters-per-hectare range. There's no need to take steps to limit the crop here; indeed, Lafon wishes he could produce more" (Tanzer). Hedonism's listing states the **2014 Perrières came in at 21 hl/ha** [MED — retailer, but consistent with Tanzer].
- **Farming.** Organic 1995, biodynamic across the domaine from 1998 (Tanzer; importers); "All the harvest and work in the vineyards are done by hand" (Kellogg). Certification: Becky Wasserman lists "Organic (certified), biodynamic methods" [HIGH]. Horse ploughing: NOT FOUND for Perrières specifically.
- **Picking order.** Lafon told Tanzer "he normally picks his various parcels of Perrières on the same day," and that "both his Perrières and his Genevrières need to be harvested earlier than his Charmes" — a direct reversal of Pierre Morey (his father's sharecropper), who "believed that Perrières and Genevrières should be picked late." Since the 2006 "eye-opener" Lafon "has frequently been among the first group of Meursault growers to harvest, 'but not as early as Arnaud Ente.'" Perrières "was typically bottled between 13.3% and 14% during the '80s and '90s but today it's more like 12.8% to 13.5%." "Lafon has not acidified his Perrières since 2003" and "has barely chaptalized Perrières in the past ten years."
- **History of the holding.** Comte Jules Lafon (who married Marie Boch on St Vincent's day 1894) assembled the domaine's Meursault/Volnay plots and bought Montrachet in 1919 (JM overview) [HIGH]. Whether the Perrières parcels date to Jules: NOT FOUND, but the 1955 planting under René Lafon (who took over 1956 and "with the share-croppers' help set about improving and replanting the vines," Nanson 2010) implies the land was already Lafon's [MED]. Dominique's timeline (Tanzer): harvest help from 1982 while working for Becky Wasserman; responsible for winemaking 1984–85 with brother Bruno; full-time from August 1986; told the sharecroppers in 1984 he would not renew their nine-year contracts, so "by 1993, Dominique was vinifying and bottling virtually all of the estate's production." (JM says "in charge since 1985"; Nanson says "took over from René in 1984." The brief's "1987" is not supported by any source found — the 1987 date is when he bought a new bottling machine, per Tanzer.) [HIGH on the sequence.]

### B2. Winemaking for Lafon Perrières (regime c. 2014)

Sources: Tanzer 2019 (with Lafon), JM overview (2026), importer sheets (Becky Wasserman, Grand Cru Selections, Kellogg), Nanson 2010/2015.

- **Pressing.** Whole clusters, bladder (pneumatic) press — "his father was the first winemaker in Meursault to switch from the traditional vertical press to a bladder press and … was always against Vaselin presses because he found them too harsh" (Tanzer) [HIGH]. Since 2009, on Denis Dubourdieu's advice, **the last ~10% of press juice is hyperoxidised separately** (its polyphenols and sotolon precursors precipitate) then blended back "along with a bit of additional sulfur"; the first 90%, "the unsqueezed juice," is rich in glutathione. Lafon: Dubourdieu convinced him that with white wines "there's a time for air and there's a time for protection against oxygen" [HIGH].
- **Settling.** "Light débourbage" of about 24 hours in temperature-controlled tank [HIGH].
- **Fermentation.** Indigenous yeasts (commercial strains only to rescue stuck ferments). Since 2010 (trialled 2009), all whites **start fermenting in stainless steel for two or three days before going to barrel** — a Dubourdieu idea; a 2013 side-by-side confirmed the new method gave "more thiols, which can bring added freshness." Alcoholic fermentation "can last anywhere from one to three months … normally 90% finished … by the end of November and completely finished by Christmas" [HIGH].
- **Barrels/new oak.** "No more than 30% new for the Perrières and Charmes" (Tanzer 2019); Lafon used 100% new through '89/'90 like his father, and "as recently as 2000 … 70% new oak for his Perrières and Charmes." JM's overview still says "up to 70 per cent for Charmes and Perrières" (a legacy figure); importers say 25–40% (Grand Cru Selections) or 50–70% (Kellogg). **For 2014, ≤30% new is the best-supported figure** (direct from Lafon to Tanzer) [HIGH]. Cooper: NOT FOUND for the domaine's Meursaults.
- **Élevage and the deep cellar.** The domaine "benefits from a deep and normally cold cellar" beneath the Clos de la Barre house (built by the Boch family, 1869 — JM) — Kellogg: "Keeping the cellars cold ensures fermentations can be slow." Regime: first racking "in the summer, without exposure to air," into barriques "at least four or five years old"; all premiers crus and Montrachet "spend a second winter in barrel" (JM); cuvées "assembled in tanks during the second winter, where they are allowed to settle for up to three months prior to bottling. Lafon says that he used to do a shorter settling in tank and that that was a mistake." Total: 16 months (Grand Cru Selections) to "18-22 months" (Becky Wasserman) — Montrachet 18–21 months (JM). **René Lafon's legendary two-plus-year élevages are history:** "René Lafon would often keep the wines for much longer in barrel" (JM) [HIGH].
- **Bâtonnage.** "Essentially stopped doing batonnage after 1999; nowadays he will do light batonnage if he feels it's necessary to help the fermentations finish or when his wines show strong reduction in barrel" (Tanzer); Nanson (2010) records the domaine "began rolling the barrels to avoid introducing more air but still getting the fine lees into suspension" [HIGH].
- **Malolactic.** Now early — "the malos now often start soon after the alcoholic fermentations" because the warmer soils mean the cellar chills later; Lafon dislikes an unprotected gap between AF and MLF. **In 2014 specifically:** "All had quick malos and were often done in Jan/Feb" (Lafon to Nanson, 23 Oct 2015) [HIGH].
- **SO2.** Bottled with "about 35 parts per million … free sulfur; until the early 2000s, it was just 20 or 25" [HIGH].
- **Fining/filtration.** Fined only when needed, in tank, with far less bentonite/casein than René; very light DE filtration from 2001, replaced in 2012 by a gentler lenticular filtration; bottled by gravity; nitrogen-rinsed bottles from the 2012s, targeting "barely one milligram per liter of oxygen" [HIGH].
- **Closure.** **DIAM 30 from the 2013 vintage** (so the 2014 is under DIAM 30); from 2015 the beeswax-emulsion DIAM 30, "slightly less reductive" [HIGH]. Earlier fix: from 2004, unbleached paraffin-only corks (no silicone).
- **Bottling date for 2014.** Not stated; per regime, spring/early summer 2016 (second winter in barrel, then up to 3 months in tank). Nanson on 23 Oct 2015: "No racking yet for these wines, except the Monthelie" [HIGH on regime; date inferred, MED].
- **Premox history and what the domaine did.** Lafon had no problems in 1995–96; "his first troubled vintage was 1999," which he called "fragile" (a barrel shortage forced him to discard more bourbes); also "2005 as a vintage with some problems … some bad bottles in 2001, 2004 and 2006. Beginning with his '07s, by most accounts, problems with premox have been increasingly rare." He maintained the incidence was "random, although more common in some vintages than in others." Jancis Robinson (2015) noted "some earlier vintages aged 'dangerously fast.'" JM: "the issue appears now to be sorted here." The fixes are listed above (no bâtonnage, paraffin corks 2004, Dubourdieu 2009: hyperoxidation + tank starts + early malos, nitrogen rinse 2012, DIAM 2013, 35 ppm free SO2) [HIGH].
- **Style.** Jancis (2015) contrasts Lafon's "more generous, open, more obviously fruity approach" with Coche's, noting he "permitted 'a little step towards le matchstick style'" only with a 2013 négociant Meursault [HIGH]. Tanzer: "Lafon's Perrières is typically citric and tensile in the early going, sometimes showing a note of moderately ripe pineapple, and more reserved than his Charmes."

### B3. The 2014 vintage at Lafon

- **Season (Jasper Morris, 2014 Vintage Overview):** mild winter, warm March with exceptionally early budbreak, cool May with frost fears, very hot Whitsun with fast flowering; then **"the clouds rolled in on Saturday 28th June bringing several waves of hail once again trashing the vineyards of Meursault, Volnay, Pommard and Beaune."** Cool, wet July–August; Drosophila suzukii "a plague." Picking began "over the weekend of 6th/7th September" with the "Grand Départ" from 13 September. Whites: "the most consistently fine white wine vintage for a generation," with "concentrated very fresh fruit and relatively high acidity" and "low risk" of premox [HIGH].
- **Hail geography (critical for Perrières):** Nanson: "The southern stretches of Meursault lost hardly anything but closer to the village and to its north losses were up to 70%." ### B4. Stories, people, connections (Perrières-specific)

- **The Pierre Morey link.** Pierre Morey was Lafon's most important sharecropper — he farmed Lafon's Montrachet until 1991 (JM) and, per Tanzer, pushed for late picking of Perrières and Genevrières, producing the "classic old Lafon style" of rich, buttery Meursault. When the métayage ended, Morey (from 1989 régisseur at Leflaive) built his own domaine — including 0.52 ha of Perrières-Dessous he had farmed and then bought in the late 1980s. Dominique's early-picking Perrières is thus a deliberate reversal of his father's sharecropper's philosophy [HIGH].
- **Becky Wasserman.** Dominique's day job in 1982–86 was at Becky Wasserman's brokerage; her company remains the domaine's export agent today [HIGH].
- **Denis Dubourdieu**, the Bordeaux white-wine professor, consulted from 2009 until his death in 2016 — an unusual Bordeaux–Burgundy bridge [HIGH].
- **Roulot.** In 2010 Lafon and "his friend and neighbor Jean-Marc Roulot" jointly took on the ex-René Manuel vines (Porusots, Bouchères) after American investors bought the domaine from Labouré-Roi — completing Lafon's set of all six central premiers crus from Perrières to Goutte d'Or (JM). Roulot's Perrières is also in Dessous, in a parcel shared with Matrot [HIGH].
- **Coche.** Lafon's ex-Gauffroy plot is physically "next to Coche-Dury's Perrières" (Tanzer) — the two wines at the dinner share a boundary [HIGH].
- **Succession.** Dominique "officially retired in December 2021, handing over to his daughter Léa and his nephew Pierre" (JM). The 2014 is fully a Dominique wine [HIGH].
- **Confrérie/Trois Glorieuses.** La Paulée de Meursault was "formally organized in the mid-1920s by the Count Lafon and Jacques Prieur" — Comte Jules Lafon — and forms one of Les Trois Glorieuses alongside the Hospices auction and the Clos de Vougeot dinner of the Confrérie des Chevaliers du Tastevin; Dominique still helps organise it (Wikipedia) [MED]. This is a direct, ready-made link between the Lafon name and the Confrérie's November weekend.
- **Perrières as Lafon's own benchmark.** Nanson's blind dozen in 2015: "the best for me from this selection was actually the 2011 from Lafon – I could drink it every day," and "Clearly an outstanding 11 … very much the most direct, lithe and mineral, cliché Perrières." Jasper Morris, meanwhile, confesses that between Lafon's Genevrières and Perrières, despite the ~40% market premium for Perrières, "if forced to choose, I might very well opt for Genevrières" [HIGH].

## PART C — DOMAINE COCHE-DURY, MEURSAULT 1ER CRU PERRIÈRES 2014

### C1. Coche's plot within Perrières

- **Sector.** "Two plots in the upper section of Perrières, one each in Perrières Dessus and Aux Perrières" (Jasper Morris, Inside Burgundy overview, updated Sep 2022; same in Sotheby's 2023: "one in the upper part known as Les Perrières Dessus and one in a central section, which goes under the name of Aux Perrières") [HIGH]. Decanter (Curtis, 2022): "Georges Coche added the holdings in Premier Cru Meursault-Perrières (two parcels, both in Perrières-Dessus)" [HIGH — minor disagreement on whether the second parcel is Aux Perrières or Dessus; both are the upper tier]. Nanson: "most of their vines lie in the higher Perrières-Dessus section." Tanzer places Lafon's ex-Gauffroy plot "next to Coche-Dury's Perrières, in the lowest part of Perrières-Dessus."
- **Size.** 0.6 ha (Jasper Morris 2023; Decanter 2022; Hedonism) [HIGH]. Winehog (2011) listed 0.5 ha; Ficofi says 0.67 ha; Wikipedia's "0.5 acres" is a unit error [MED on the spread]. Jasper: 0.6 ha "puts Domaine Coche-Dury inside the top 10 owners by size – and of course the domaine is at the very top of the tree as far as quality is concerned."
- **Vine age.** Winehog's 2011 table: **Perrières planted 1950, 1970, 2005**, on limestone [MED — single source, but specific]. Hedonism: "0.6 hectares of very old vines" [LOW]. In 2014 the oldest block was 64 years old.
- **Acquisition.** By Georges Coche (who ran his share of Léon Coche's domaine from 1964; Jean-François took over in 1973) — so pre-1973 [HIGH]. Jean-François's own additions were Volnay Taillepieds/Clos des Chênes and, in 1985, the Corton-Charlemagne métayage (Decanter).
- **Farming.** Not organic, not biodynamic ("The viticulture is neither organic nor biodynamic" — Jasper 2023; "meticulously looked after, though are not organic" — Jasper 2021, noting Raphaël would sometimes prefer a synthetic contact product over repeated passages). All vineyards now ploughed (JM 2021); soil "now worked manually" (Decanter). No clones — "old vines for massal selection," vine-by-vine replanting (Decanter; Ficofi). Raphaël is sceptical of Guyot-Poussard pruning ("Each individual vine needs its own decision"). Yields low "mainly because the vines are old, and not because of green harvesting" (Decanter); JFC's dictum: "green harvesting is already too late" (Nanson 2007); Ficofi cites yields "as low as 25 hl/ha" [HIGH].
- **Picking.** "Picking is generally early to retain freshness" (Decanter) [HIGH]. Coche's 2014 harvest dates: NOT FOUND.

### C2. Why Coche Perrières is the most sought Meursault of all

- Jasper Morris: "Domaine Coche-Dury is arguably the most internationally sought-after of all white Burgundy producers"; on Perrières: "the exceptionally talented Jean-François Coche-Dury, who is the absolute leader of Perrières winemakers." His honest answer to *why*: "Are the yields low? Not exceptionally. Is there some wizardry during the vinification or élevage … specific techniques are not really the issue here: sometimes I think it is just that Jean-François Coche-Dury has the green fingers of a natural gardener" [HIGH].
- Decanter (2022): "These wines sell for several times that of even their most illustrious fellow vignerons in the village … Coche-Dury undisputedly leads the pack." Auction: Perrières from the same vintage as the 1986 Corton-Charlemagne "averaged roughly £3,200 a bottle last year, while 10 years ago it traded for just over £760 a bottle." Robert Parker's 1980s line that Jean-François was "one of the greatest winemakers on planet earth" (Decanter) and the Parker-site formulation Nanson quotes: "With virtually universal agreement among connoisseurs of dry white wines, Jean-Francois Coche is the greatest producer of white Burgundy. If you disagree, it probably means that you have never encountered Coche's wines" [HIGH]. Clive Coates: "one of the finest white wine domaines in the world"; Jancis: "certainly the most reliable source of great white Burgundies" (via Wikipedia) [MED].
- Nanson (2007) reminds that the reputation predates Parker: "Jean-François' father, Georges, had entered many competitions with the domaine's wines and his collection of gold medals had already ensured that many top restaurants already listed the wines in an era before marks out of 100 were considered de rigeur" [HIGH].
- The rationing/secrecy mythology (Jancis, 2015): "they closed their visitor's book years ago"; appointments "at six in the evening when it is too dark to work in the vines"; "wine writers are no longer allowed to taste wines from barrel" after an "unfortunate experience"; "Not for the Coches winemaker dinners around the globe. They prefer to stay within spitting distance of their barrels." JM (2021): "No changes are made at this domaine without an enormous degree of reflection and testing." [HIGH]
- Scarcity: production ~3,500–4,200 cases total (Wikipedia; winehog); 0.6 ha of Perrières at ~25–35 hl/ha is roughly 6–9 barrels — call it 1,800–2,700 bottles in a normal year, fewer in the "short vintages of 2011–2014 inclusive" (JM 2021) [MED, arithmetic].

### C3. Winemaking for Coche Perrières — and the 2014 regime

**Who made it.** Raphaël Coche (b. c. 1972; began working alongside his father at fifteen — JM; formally 1997/1999 after oenology studies; full winemaking responsibility on Jean-François's official retirement in 2010; runs the domaine with his wife Charline). The 2014 is a Raphaël wine, with Jean-François still present in the cellar. Raphaël: "My father and I see eye-to-eye on winemaking" (Decanter) [HIGH].

**Crushing and pressing — the signature.** "Vines are firmly crushed before pressing, using an old-style hydraulic Vaslin press, which must be a factor in the Coche-Dury style" (Jasper Morris, 2023; JM overview: "firmly crushed before pressing, which must surely be a factor"). Decanter: "The grapes are lightly crushed, just enough to liberate the juice during pressing." A **pneumatic press arrived in 2009** after exhaustive trials ("It might not have been much fun being the pneumatic press salesman … with the amount of trials that Jean-François Coche in particular would have insisted on") and "is now used as well as the traditional Vaslin press. Raphaël remains a fan of the latter, 'ours is a Formula One version', but noting that you have to have enough volume of a given cuvée to fill the press if you use a Vaslin" (JM 2021). Raphaël's rationale for the pneumatic (Decanter): "how best to obtain the discrete, elegantly bitter notes from the phenolics in the grape skins that compensate for acidity in a warm or concentrated vintage, such as 2015 or 2016." **Which press the 2014 Perrières saw is NOT FOUND**; given Raphaël's preference for the Vaslin when volume allows and 2014's small crop, either is plausible [LOW]. **On the brief's "high SO2 at pressing" hypothesis: NOT FOUND in any source retrieved.** The closest statements are Jasper's "the wines receive their requisite dose of sulphur" (2023) and Bestofwines' contrary "bottled unfiltered with minimal sulfur additions" [LOW]. Present it as a widely repeated trade theory (crushing + sulfured must + long lees contact → sulfide-driven "gunflint"), not as a sourced fact.

**Reduction — "beneficial reduction."** Jasper Morris (2021): "Other domaines also spring to mind when discussing the reductive struck match/gun flint character of some practitioners today, but Coche-Dury is one of the most frequently referenced producers. I have taken to calling this style beneficial reduction, as long as it is not taken to extremes. I have the impression that Raphaël has somewhat backed off this dimension: he works with fewer lees and with less trituration … effectively … a mashing up of the skins – since employing the pneumatic press." Jancis (2015) credits Coche with having "instigated the worldwide trend towards the reductive 'struck match' flavours in white wines" and observes Raphaël making "very slightly fruitier, more accessible wines than his father." Decanter: "Raphaël seems less obsessed with searching for a reductive note in the wines than his father had been." Jasper on the 2011 Perrières: "an ideal degree of gunflint reduction, steely rather than struck match." Winehog on the house style: "the assertive bouquet of gun powder and hints of popcorn – which can be very distinctive when the wines are young" [HIGH].

**New oak — the "50%" question, verified.** Figures found, with date of statement:
- Nanson, 2007: "not such a high new oak percentage (around 50%), but given an average 22 months in barrel it is time enough for the oak to flex its muscles. Seasoned observers point to a little less oak influence on young wines post-1999."
- Winehog, 2011: "generally Coche-Dury uses quite a lot new oak. In some years they have used up to 50% new casks on the top wines."
- Jasper Morris, Inside Burgundy overview (2022) & Sotheby's (2023): "a relatively low percentage of new wood, no more than 25 per cent except for the very top wines."
- Decanter/Curtis, 2022 (after visiting Raphaël): he "has reduced the proportion of new wood to 20%-25% for most wines."
- Ficofi (undated, c. 2024): "aged 18 months in barrel (20% new oak)."
- Winewithseth: "up to 50 percent for Corton-Charlemagne in some vintages" [LOW].
**Confidence range for the 2014 Perrières: 25–50% new oak, most likely c. 30–50% since Perrières counts among "the very top wines" and the reductions Curtis describes are Raphaël-era trends still in progress in 2014** [MED]. Cooper: **Tonnellerie Damy** (Meursault) — and unusually, "At Coche-Dury, they select and purchase their own wood, dry it themselves, and then supply it to Tonnellerie Damy to turn into barrels" (JM 2021) [HIGH]. Barrel rotation is therefore committed two years ahead: 2014's new barrels were reused for 2016, 2018, etc.

**Élevage.** Fermented and matured in barrel; "Whites are racked in July, when the barrels of a given cuvée are blended, then returned to cask for a second winter to await their turn to go into bottle, cask by cask in April of their second year" (Jasper, 2023) — i.e., c. 18–19 months; other sources say 15–22 months (winehog), 15 months "to almost two years according to the vintage" (Decanter), "over two winters at least" (Decanter). "Fermentation is also long, and the malolactic conversion is slow." Raphaël: **"There is no recipe – the élevage depends upon the life the vine has lived during the season"** (Decanter) [HIGH]. Bâtonnage: historically "quite frequent" (winehog 2011); now "less bâtonnage" and "fewer lees" under Raphaël (Decanter; JM) [HIGH].

**Fining/filtration/bottling.** "Fined with egg whites and bottled, primarily by hand with an instrument called a chèvre à deux becs (a double spigot)" — the Henri Jayer method, max eight casks a day; JFC: "without pushing the wine or working it too much, aerating it, or subjecting it to hot or cold temperature … the wine must be manipulated as little as possible." Corks hand-selected, blemish-free side to the wine (Decanter). A bottling line now exists "used for some wines though not necessarily the same ones each year" (JM). Unfiltered (winehog; Ficofi). Closure: natural cork (no DIAM reported) [HIGH]. Bottling date for 2014 Perrières: by regime, April 2016 [MED].

**Premox record.** The gdeschamps.net oxidised-Burgundy wiki lists sporadic Coche failures (1992 Corton-Charlemagne — "all 9 bottles oxidized"; a 1999 Rougeots; 2007 Rougeots "on the fast highway to premoxville") but "many bottles from these same vintages showed no oxidation"; Nanson's 2007 dinner had a 1990 village "Ooops – oxidised" [MED]. No 2014 premox reports found. Coche's high-reduction, high-lees style is generally regarded as protective; the domaine has not adopted DIAM.

**Reds, for context only:** destemmed, 10–15% whole cluster under Raphaël after a 2–3-day cold soak (Decanter).

## PART D — HEAD-TO-HEAD TALKING POINTS FOR THE TABLE

1. **Same climat, different tiers, adjoining plots.** Lafon is mostly in Perrières-Dessous (the sector locals call the best, on the "Grande Perrière" beside Genevrières-Dessus and just south of the Clos); Coche is in Perrières-Dessus/Aux Perrières, the higher, stonier, more "cutting" terraces above the old quarry. Lafon's small ex-Gauffroy plot literally borders Coche's. Essa's shorthand: Dessous = civilised, opulent, salin/iodé; Dessus = "vin de cailloux sur des terres maigres," almost cutting on the tongue.
2. **Two philosophies of oxygen.** Lafon (with Dubourdieu): bladder press, hyperoxidised press fraction, tank-started ferments, minimal bâtonnage, nitrogen-rinsed bottles, DIAM 30, 35 ppm free SO2 — the most engineered anti-premox protocol in Meursault. Coche: firm crushing, Vaslin (plus pneumatic from 2009), long lees, egg-white fining, hand bottling cask by cask, natural cork — reduction as the protective philosophy ("beneficial reduction" — Jasper).
3. **New oak.** Lafon ≤30% (2014); Coche 25–50% (top wines), Damy barrels from Coche's own wood.
4. **Élevage.** Both two winters; Lafon finishes in tank for up to three months, Coche bottles straight from cask in April.
5. **2014.** Both spared by the 28 June hail (southern Meursault); Lafon 21 hl/ha from 59-year-old massal vines; Coche's 2011–14 were "short vintages" that pushed Raphaël into buying grapes from 2015. Scores: Lafon 93–97 (Tanzer/Galloni/Martin 97, WA 96, JM 95, BH 94); Coche WA 97, BH 96.
6. **Price gap** (~4×) is a market statement about the producer, not the tier; the locals' preferred tier is the one Lafon is in.
7. **Grand cru.** Lavalle's tête de cuvée, refused in the 1930s because the village wanted "Meursault" on the label (Essa) / because village committees differed (Raphaël Coche: "Tout est sous-classé à Meursault"); the Bardets' Clos dossier stalls because Dessous owners want the whole lower tier in and Dessus owners (Coche, Bouchard) would be out.
8. **Confrérie hooks.** Comte Jules Lafon co-founded La Paulée (mid-1920s), the Meursault leg of the Trois Glorieuses with the Clos de Vougeot dinner; Coche's 1979 Perrières wore a Tastevinage label approved in 1981.
