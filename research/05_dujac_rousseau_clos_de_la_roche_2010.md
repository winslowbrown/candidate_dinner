# PART A — THE CLIMAT: CLOS DE LA ROCHE GRAND CRU

## A1. Location, size, exposure, altitude, soil

- Commune: Morey-Saint-Denis, Côte de Nuits; northernmost of Morey's grands crus, touching the Gevrey-Chambertin boundary (Latricières-Chambertin/Combottes side) to the north and Clos Saint-Denis to the south; the Route des Grands Crus marks the eastern edge. [HIGH — Wikipedia EN; Sorting Table tech sheet]
- Size: **16.90 ha** is the figure used by Decanter (Curtis, 2024), Rare Wine Invest and Lambier; French Wikipedia gives 16.55 ha; English Wikipedia gives a stale 13.41 ha (2008 production data). Use **16.9 ha, largest grand cru in Morey**. [HIGH]
- Altitude ~250 m (fr.wikipedia); east/"levant" exposure. [MED]
- Geology (Decanter/Curtis 2024): upper slope on Prémeaux limestone; lower slope on slightly older crinoidal (entroque) limestone; limestone pebbles in Monts Luisants; alluvial fans; more topsoil mid-slope; the limestone bedrock "periodically breaks through the surface". [MED-HIGH]
- fr.wikipedia: "calcaire (30 cm de terre), peu de cailloutis", with large blocks of stone ("rocher") that gave the vineyard its name. [MED]
- Regulatory: Pinot Noir; 35 hl/ha base yield; minimum density 9,000 vines/ha; 11.5% minimum potential (en.wikipedia). Production ~589 hl/yr ≈ 78,500 bottles (fr.wikipedia). [MED]

## A2. Name etymology

"Roche" = the rock: the limestone outcrops/large stone blocks that surface in the original lieu-dit. The Sorting Table sheet: name derived "from the presence of rocks that show on the surface of this soil". There is no surviving wall — "Clos" here is historical/nominal (see also en.wikipedia's generic "clos" gloss). [HIGH on the rock etymology; the "no wall" point is common knowledge — Curtis does not contradict it, but I did not fetch a specific source: mark MED]

Older folk etymologies (Druidic altar, "roche" as a sacrificial stone) circulate in trade literature; NOT FOUND in any source fetched here — do not cite.

## A3. Classification history and expansion (the "quadrupling")

From Decanter (Charles Curtis MW, 21 Oct 2024) [HIGH for the sequence; figures MED]:
- **Original lieu-dit Clos de la Roche: 4.57 ha.**
- **1936 INAO decree**: the grand cru AOC was drawn to include not just the lieu-dit but also **Les Mochamps, Les Froichots, Les Fremières, Les Chabiots and the lower part of Monts Luisants**, giving ~15.34 ha.
- **1971**: further additions from **Les Genavrières (0.88 ha) and Les Chaffots (0.07 ha)**, arriving at ~16.9 ha.
- Curtis: the appellation "quadrupled in area", which still "generates debate among connoisseurs about terroir quality" — i.e. how much of today's Clos de la Roche is really "the rock".
- fr.wikipedia dates the AOC to 1936 (with the usual boilerplate about the 1910 mildew crisis).

Lieux-dits within today's grand cru (Curtis): **Clos de la Roche (the original), Mochamps, Froichots, Fremières, Chabiots, Monts Luisants (lower part), Genavrières, Chaffots.** The brief's list is confirmed (Les Mochamps, Monts Luisants, Les Froichots, Les Fremières, Les Chaffots, Les Genavrières) plus **Les Chabiots** which the brief omitted — and Chabiots matters because Dujac owns 0.59 ha of it.

Talking point: **Dujac's ~0.5 ha and Rousseau's ~0.48 ha in the original lieu-dit are the "true rock" components; the rest of both holdings is 1936/1971 annexed ground** (Monts Luisants/Chabiots for Dujac; Fremières for Rousseau). Ponsot, by contrast, has ~2/3 of its Clos de la Roche in the original climat and 1/3 in "Clos des Monts-Luisants" at the foot of the slope (domaine-ponsot.com). [HIGH]

## A4. Neighbours and how the site differs

- North: Gevrey-Chambertin (Latricières-Chambertin, Aux Combottes 1er cru — which Dujac also owns, 1.15 ha). Clos de la Roche is often described as the most "Gevrey-like" of Morey's grands crus: darker fruit, more muscle and tannic grip than Clos Saint-Denis. Curtis's summary of the wine: "full-bodied, rich, black-fruit aromas predominant, firm muscular texture, tannic grip, savoury/gamey element (lower reaches), dark and brooding in youth, seductively expressive with age." [HIGH as a critic's characterisation]
- South: Clos Saint-Denis (Dujac also owns 1.29–1.45 ha) — finer, more floral/silky; the classic Dujac side-by-side.
- Ponsot's Alexandre Abel to Curtis: "The lieu-dit Clos de la Roche gives substance, while Les Monts Luisants brings" freshness. [HIGH]
- Winehog (Steen Öhman, Rousseau CDLR 2011 note): "quite different from the Gevrey wines… deep and pure fruit, more roundly structured and finely balanced with a quite powerful acidity and minerality." Berkeley & Stuart (Rousseau importer text): CDLR has "thin topsoil with prominent limestone rock… firm tannins, pronounced minerality, and more of a wild character than the wines from Gevrey." [MED]

# PART B — DOMAINE ARMAND ROUSSEAU, CLOS DE LA ROCHE GRAND CRU 2010

## B1. The producer's plot

- **Size: 1.48 ha** in **two parcels**, vinified together. [HIGH — Wikipedia; Coates/Decanter 2011; Rare Wine Invest 2022; Lambier]
- **Location**: Curtis (Decanter 2024): "a plot at the southern extremity of lieu-dit Clos de la Roche and another larger one (almost 1 ha) in Fremières." WHWC (retailer, 2022 vintage) breaks it as ~1 ha Les Fremières + ~0.48 ha original Clos de la Roche. [MED-HIGH]
  - Les Fremières lies on the lower/east side, straddling the Route des Grands Crus side of the appellation (below the original clos) — i.e. Rousseau's larger parcel is lower-slope, 1936-annexed ground; the smaller is in the "rock". [MED — inferred from the lieu-dit position described by Curtis; treat the slope-position claim as MED]
- **Acquisition date — sources conflict:**
  - Bill Nanson, Burgundy-Report profile (July 2005): Clos de la Roche acquired **1920** (his list: Charmes 1919, CDLR 1920, Chambertin 1921/1943/1956, Mazis 1937, Mazoyères 1940, Bèze 1954, Clos St-Jacques 1954, Ruchottes 1977). [MED]
  - domaine-rousseau.com history page: Armand, after his 1909 marriage, "acquired prestigious parcels including Charmes-Chambertin, Clos de la Roche, and Chambertin", then Mazy and Mazoyères in the 1930s. → consistent with a 1920s Armand-era first purchase. [HIGH that Armand bought the first piece]
  - Wikipedia EN: "Acquired in portions: initial purchase in late 1910s–1920s, then 1.48 hectares in 1965 and 1975." Decanter (Coates, 2011) and WineWiki: "acquired 1965 and again 1975".
  - **Reconciliation [MED]:** Armand bought the first Clos de la Roche piece c. 1920; Charles enlarged/reshaped the holding with purchases in 1965 and 1975 to reach the present 1.48 ha in two parcels. Say it this way.
- **Vine age / replanting**: Neal Martin (Wine Advocate #210, Dec 2013, on the 2012): "part of the vineyard was replanted five years ago" → a replanting c. **2008**; in 2010 those young vines were 2 years old and would not have been in the grand cru (young vines typically excluded or declassified — NOT CONFIRMED for Rousseau specifically). Otherwise vine age NOT FOUND. [MED]
- **Farming**: no herbicides; insecticides stopped c. 2000 (Sotheby's); "not biodynamic" (Coates 2011); the domaine's own site describes mechanical soil work, green harvest, leaf removal, yields 30–40 hl/ha; Frederick Wildman lists "organic/biodynamic/sustainable practices" but there is **no organic certification** (Sotheby's: "the estate lacks organic certification"). Guyot; ~11–12,000 vines/ha (WineWiki/Sotheby's). [MED-HIGH]
- Charles Rousseau's famous massale-selection vines: Coates: "very fine vines from exceptional sélection massale." [MED]

**Confidence range for Rousseau Clos de la Roche 2010: 0–10% whole cluster, with 0% (fully destemmed) most likely. HIGH confidence that it was ≥90% destemmed; MED-HIGH that it was 100% destemmed.**
Reasoning: (1) Coates, writing in 2011 with direct access to Eric Rousseau, records that the domaine "normally" destems, with 2009's 15% whole cluster being a noted exception — and even that exception is described in the context of Chambertin/Bèze; (2) every source that assigns stems to Rousseau assigns them to the top Gevrey wines or to the post-2015 era; (3) Curtis (2024) states flatly that the Clos de la Roche is destemmed; (4) nothing places stems in the 2010 CDLR. The brief's "historically ~10–20% stems for Chambertin/Bèze only" is **consistent** with the 2005 "90% destemmed" figure and the 2009 "15%" experiment, but I could not find a source that says this in exactly those words for 2010 — say "a small proportion in the top Gevrey crus in some years, none documented for Clos de la Roche".

## B4. Stories, people, connections — Rousseau

- **Armand Rousseau (1884–1959)**: from a family of growers, coopers and merchants; inherited his first vines at 18 (1902) and gained the house and more vines on marriage in 1909; began as a wine broker/bulk seller; on the advice of **Raymond Baudoin** (founder of *La Revue du Vin de France*) began domaine-bottling for restaurateurs and private clients in the early 1930s — one of the first in Burgundy — and exported to the US after Repeal (1934–35) via Frank Schoonmaker's circle [the Schoonmaker link is common knowledge, NOT FOUND in fetched sources — MED]. Killed in a car accident in 1959. [HIGH]
- **Charles Rousseau (1923–2016)**: studied law and oenology at Dijon; joined 1945/46; took over at 36 in 1959 with ~6.5 ha and built it to 14+ ha (Clos de Bèze 1961 via the Nousbaum purchase, Clos St-Jacques 1954, Clos des Ruchottes 1977/78 from Thomas-Bassot, Chambertin additions 1968/1983, Clos de la Roche 1965/1975). Fluent in English and German, he built the export markets. On Chambertin vs Bèze: "Le Chambertin is male, well-built… Clos de Bèze is more complex, classier and delicate." On 1986: "You should have seen my vineyards in 1986. The ground was carpeted with rotten berries" — 50 harvesters, 12 days of triage. [HIGH]
- **Eric Rousseau (b. 1957)**: Lycée Agricole et Viticole de Mâcon-Davayé, oenology at Dijon; joined 1982; in charge of vinification since the early 1990s; introduced green harvest, leaf-thinning, mechanical soil work, stopped insecticides (2000). [HIGH]
- **Cyrielle Rousseau (b. 1988)**: degrees in geology, viticulture and wine trade (DTO) from Dijon; harvests in Oregon, Australia, New Zealand; involved from the 2012 vintage, full-time from 2014; now co-runs the domaine, does the tastings with "her brace of dogs" (Nanson, Nov 2025). Her nickname for the Clos de la Roche: "I call this Jean-Paul after the robots of JP Gautier" (to Nanson, 2022 — i.e., the armoured, corseted wine of the range). On 2021: "About half a harvest… It was like the seven plagues of Egypt." On Ruchottes: "It's my favourite too – it's where my roots are!" [HIGH]
- **Frédéric Robert**: commercial director (with Charles's daughter Brigitte; another daughter Corinne joined the office in 1993 with a Dijon oenology diploma). Note: the brief says "Frédéric Rousseau" — the Frédéric at the domaine is **Frédéric Robert**, not a Rousseau. [HIGH]
- Berkeley & Stuart's importer page names Eric's daughters as "Cirille and Elodie" — spelling errors/inconsistent with all other sources; ignore.
- Rousseau's style vs Dujac's, in Curtis's words (2024): Rousseau's CDLR wines "seem lighter, with more purity of fruit but less complexity" than the stemmy/oaky neighbours — a line to deploy carefully at the table.
- **Importer: Frederick Wildman & Sons, New York** — which was also Dujac's first US importer (from 1970, Nanson 2009). Shared-importer connection between the two wines. [HIGH]
- Confrérie des Chevaliers du Tastevin: no specific Rousseau connection found in fetched sources — NOT FOUND.

[MED-HIGH]

- Yield target: ~6–7 bunches per vine, ~35 hl/ha (Nanson 2009, 2014). [HIGH]

**Confidence range for Dujac Clos de la Roche 2010: 80–90% whole cluster. HIGH confidence.**
Reasoning: Galloni's Dec 2011 producer note is a direct report of Jeremy Seysses's 2010 regime, and it names the exceptions (Charmes and Combottes ~70%; Chambertin and RSV 100%); Clos de la Roche is not among the exceptions, so it sits in the 80–90% band. This is corroborated by Jeremy's contemporaneous "over 80% every year" (SommChat) and by his stated logic (Gevrey sites get more destemming; Morey grands crus with older vines stay whole). If pressed for a single number, **~85%** is the defensible midpoint; do not claim 100%.

Why not 100% in 2010? 2010 bunches were loose with shot berries (millerandage) — good for stem-inclusion in principle (small berries, high skin ratio) — but potential alcohols were low (12–12.5%) and the wines were chaptalised; Jeremy's "high acid vintages, it helps round things out" logic argues for high but not total inclusion. [MED — interpretive]

# PART D — TASTING FRAMEWORK FOR THE PAIR (what to say, what to look for)

1. **Aromatics**: Dujac's 80–90% stems + ~90–100% new oak in 2010 → expect the "whole-cluster dimension" Nanson finds in the Echézeaux 2010: sandalwood, dried flowers, tea, sweet spice; Tanzer's "candied raspberry, flowers, stone and sandalwood". Rousseau destemmed, zero new oak → Meadows' "anise, earth, underbrush, violets", Tanzer's "crushed rock, musky minerality, brown spices, leather"; Jancis's "a rather wild note on the end."
2. **Structure**: Galloni called the Rousseau "all about power, structure and length… huge depth and serious extract… big tannins" and the Dujac "decidedly feminine… weightless energy". Counter-intuitive: the destemmed wine is the more tannic/muscular one in 2010 (thick-skinned, small-berry fruit, 15–21-day cuvaison, no new-oak polish), the stemmed wine the more perfumed and silky (stems aerate the cap, moderate extraction). Use that reversal.
3. **Colour**: Rousseau 2012 already "quite a pale vermilion" in 2025 per Morris — expect the Rousseau 2010 to look lighter than the Dujac.
4. **Age**: Morris (Nov 2025) has the Rousseau 2010 at 97 and drinking now (2022–2032); Galloni wanted 2030 for it. Dujac: Galloni 2020–2035, BBR to 2055. Both 16 years old on 26 Oct 2026 — mid-window. Decant the Dujac for organic-era reduction.
5. **The rock**: both wines contain ~0.5 ha of the original 4.57-ha clos; the rest is Chabiots/Monts Luisants (Dujac, upper/mid) vs Fremières (Rousseau, lower). If someone asks "which is more 'Clos de la Roche'?" — Ponsot would say the original clos gives "substance" and Monts Luisants "freshness"; Rousseau's Fremières fruit sits lower and warmer.
