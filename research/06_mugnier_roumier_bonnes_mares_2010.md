# Dossier 06 — Bonnes-Mares Grand Cru 2010: Domaine Jacques-Frédéric Mugnier vs. Domaine Georges Roumier

Dinner theme: "The Importance of Being Stemmed." This pair is the cleanest stems-vs-destemmed contrast in the whole dinner: two next-door neighbours in Chambolle (the domaines share a wall on the bend of the Rue de Vergy), the same grand cru, the same vintage, and two diametrically opposed decisions at the destemmer. Mugnier: 100% destemmed, always. Roumier: partial whole-cluster, decided at the sorting table each year, typically 30-60% in the grands crus.

Compiled 5 September 2026. Confidence tags: [HIGH] = primary source or multiple concordant critic sources; [MED] = single reliable source or minor disagreement; [LOW] = single secondary source, inference, or unverified. "NOT FOUND" means I looked and could not source it.

Research note on tooling: the web-search budget for this session ran out partway through, and CellarTracker, Wine-Searcher, Vinous (current site), Burghound, Inside Burgundy note pages, Winehog premium and Decanter Premium are paywalled or bot-blocked. I compensated by pulling full text from Vinous's legacy v1 site (Neal Martin's Roumier and Mugnier articles are fully readable there), Burgundy-Report, The Fine Wine Review (Claude Kolm), the domaines' own sites and tech-sheet PDFs, Bourgogne Aujourd'hui, Wine Berserkers threads (including a May 2011 cellar-visit report from Roumier), the Underground Wine Letter's November 2011 barrel report on the 2010s, and retailer pages carrying critic notes.

## PART I — THE CLIMAT: BONNES-MARES (shared by both wines)

### 1.1 Location, size, exposure, altitude

- Bonnes-Mares straddles the Chambolle-Musigny / Morey-Saint-Denis boundary at the northern exit of Chambolle village. It is bounded to the north by Clos de Tart (Morey), to the east by the Route des Grands Crus, and to the south by Chambolle 1er cru Les Fuées and the village portion of Les Cras; the top of the slope above it is village land or the 1er cru Les Véroilles (Ghislaine Barthod monopole). [HIGH — Winehog; Wikipedia]
- Lieu-dit surface: 15 ha 5.72 a total, of which 13.5417 ha in Chambolle-Musigny and 1.5155 ha in Morey-Saint-Denis (plus cadastral parcel AR 64 of 27.8 a). Surface in production 2023: 14.92 ha; production 2023: 657 hl; 25 exploitants in 2023 (per Landrieu-Lussigny & Pitiot 2025). [HIGH — French Wikipedia citing douanes data and Pitiot]
- Neal Martin (2018) counts "22 proprietors"; Jasper Morris's booklet (2021) gives 13.54 ha in Chambolle "with the remainder across the border." [HIGH]
- Roumier's own fiche gives the estate parcel as "Exposition: Est. Pente de 15 à 20%. Altitude: de 270 à 310 mètres" — a reasonable proxy for the climat as a whole. [HIGH for Roumier's plots; MED as climat-wide]
- A curiosity: a small piece of cadastral "Bonnes Mares" lies inside the walls of Clos de Tart and is bottled as Clos de Tart. [MED — Winehog, free portion]

### 1.2 Geology and the terres rouges / terres blanches split

This is the single most important structural fact for tonight's two wines.

- "There is a diagonal path leading from the lower part of the vineyard at the southern end to the top at the Morey end. Broadly speaking the soil below this line is terres rouges; above it terres blanches, with corresponding differences in style." — Jasper Morris MW, Bonnes-Mares dinner booklet, Nov 2021. [HIGH]
- Terres blanches = white marl with crushed oyster shell (marnes à Ostrea acuminata), Bajocian/Bathonian marls, upper slope; terres rouges = crinoidal limestone (calcaire à entroques) and red clay-limestone with compact Bathonian "Prémeaux-type" bedrock, lower slope and Morey end. [HIGH — Inside Burgundy Roumier report; Roumier fiche technique]
- Roumier's fiche: "La partie supérieure du coteau 'Les Terres Blanches' est composée de marnes calcaires riches en fossile d'Ostrea Acuminata. La partie inférieure 'Les Terres Rouges' est faite d'un sous-sol argilo-calcaire avec des roches compactes du Bathonien (type calcaire de Prémeaux)." [HIGH — primary]
- TerroirSense (Chinese-language review site, overlaying Françoise Vannier-Petit's geological map) notes that "the two types of soils are not bounded by the border separating Chambolle and Morey and that the 'white soil' area is significantly smaller than the 'red soil' area." [MED]
- Stylistic reading: terres rouges gives "the power and concentration whilst the terres blanches gives the finesse and definition" — Christophe Roumier to Neal Martin (2018). Roumier's fiche: terres rouges gives "la richesse, l'onctuosité et des arômes de fruits rouges voire noirs"; terres blanches gives "l'épice, les fleurs blanches et même la pivoine rouge avec ses notes poivrées." [HIGH]
- The character question: "it does not taste entirely like a wine from Chambolle-Musigny — probably it has a touch more kinship with Morey-St-Denis, for there is definitely a wilder streak … It sits a little outside the rest of the family — 'perhaps a brother-in-law', as François Millet of Domaine Comte Georges de Vogüé has suggested." — Jasper Morris. [HIGH]
- Mugnier's own fiche: "Cette vigne située entre le village de Chambolle-Musigny et celui de Morey-saint-Denis, présente un caractère intermédiaire entre ces deux appellations. L'influence des terroirs de Morey donne à ce vin sa franchise, sa solidité, son caractère terrien." [HIGH — primary]

### 1.3 Name etymology

Three competing hypotheses, none proven [HIGH that they are the standard three]:
1. bonnes mères — the "good mothers," i.e., the Cistercian nuns of the abbey of Notre-Dame de Tart (owners of the adjacent Clos de Tart).
2. From the old verb marer / the old French marre (a curved vigneron's hoe) — "well-cultivated," possibly referencing the filling-in of old quarries (pre-Indo-European mer/mar = rock).
3. A local myth favoured by the Drouhin family: a vigneron unearthed a sculpture of three fecundity goddesses, the bonnes mères (cf. the lieu-dit "Les Bonnes Maires" at Corgoloin where such a statue was found).
Sources: Jasper Morris booklet; English and French Wikipedia (citing Pitiot/Landrieu-Lussigny).

### 1.4 Classification history

- 1816: André Jullien places Bonnes-Mares in his "première classe" but only by name. 1855: Jules Lavalle ranks it "première cuvée" — i.e., below the two "têtes de cuvée" of Morey (Clos de Tart) and Chambolle (Musigny). 1861 Beaune committee classification: part of Bonnes-Mares in 1st class, part in 2nd class. [HIGH — French Wikipedia]
- A detail worth having at the table: the AOC decree of 8 December 1936 (the same decree as Clos Saint-Denis and Clos de la Roche) covered only the 1.8455 ha of Bonnes-Mares lying in Morey-Saint-Denis. The 13.705 ha in Chambolle were added by the decree of 3 July 1944 — one of the last acts of the Vichy régime — bringing the total to 15.55 ha. [HIGH — French Wikipedia citing JORF 11 Dec 1936 and the 1944 decree]
- Cahier des charges modified October 2009 and November 2011 (maximum yield raised from 35 to 42 hl/ha; rendement butoir 49 hl/ha). Minimum density 9,000 vines/ha. [HIGH]

## PART II — WINE (a): DOMAINE JACQUES-FRÉDÉRIC MUGNIER, BONNES-MARES GRAND CRU 2010

### 2. Mugnier's plot within Bonnes-Mares

- Size: 0.36 ha (domaine: "une bande étroite de 36 ares"); Burghound gives 0.35 ha; Underground Wine Letter gives "0.89 acres." [HIGH]
- Shape and position: "a narrow strip of 36 ares, stretching the full height of the hillside" (domaine, 2024 fiche). Burghound (via Farr Vintners): "vines span from top to bottom across two soil types — terres rouges (approximately 1/3) and terres blanches (approximately 2/3)." Bill Nanson (2006 profile, from Frédéric): "A mix of terres blanche and terres rouge, Frédéric thinks of the wine character being like a more powerful Fuées — its neighbour." [HIGH that the strip runs top-to-bottom and mixes both soils; MED on the 1/3-2/3 split]
  - Two secondary sources (Underground Wine Letter 2011: "0.89 acres at the top of the slope"; WineWiki: "narrow upper-slope strip") say "top of slope." The domaine's own wording ("allongée sur toute la hauteur du coteau") and Burghound's both say full height. Trust the domaine. [HIGH]
  - Exact lateral position (which cadastral parcel; north or south end): NOT FOUND in open sources (Winehog's Terroir Insight on Mugnier Bonnes-Mares, Feb 2018, is paywalled and almost certainly has it). Frédéric's "more powerful Fuées — its neighbour" hints at the southern/Chambolle end near Fuées, but that is inference only. [LOW]
- Planting: "plantée pour moitié en 1988, le reste en 1961 et 1980" (domaine). Nanson 2006: Frédéric "was never completely satisfied with his Bonnes-Mares, assuming it might be something due to the particular mix of clones. To tackle this, in 1988 half of their 0.36 hectares of vines were replanted and only now [2006] as they reach maturity is the wine quality reaching his expectations." Thatcher's says 1987; Burghound says "50% planted in 1980-1988, with the remainder from the 1950s." Trust the domaine: 1961 / 1980 / 1988. [HIGH] In 2010 the vines were therefore 22, 30 and 49 years old; the 1988 half had just come of age.
- Production: "entre 900 et 1500 bouteilles" per year (domaine); ~100 cases (Nanson 2006); 4 barrels = 1,200 bottles in 2013 (Kolm). [HIGH]
- Acquisition: part of the original 1863 purchase of the Château de Chambolle-Musigny and its ~4 ha from the Marey-Mange/Marey-Monge family (sources disagree on the exact year the château itself was bought — 1863 per Nanson/Jockovino, 1899 per WineWiki/UWL; the domaine's own history says "Fondé en 1863"). The family's Bonnes-Mares holding was once larger: Maison Joseph Drouhin's Bonnes-Mares plots "originate from the Mugnier holdings" (1940s) per Winehog. A Clos de Vougeot parcel was also sold after the 1949 vintage. [MED]
- Farming: no fertiliser since 1986, no herbicide since 1990 (Neal Martin says 1991), no insecticide since 1995 (Martin: 1997). Never certified organic or biodynamic; Frédéric has published a sceptical essay on biodynamics ("Regard sur la biodynamie") and a long anti-pesticide essay ("Pour en finir avec les pesticides", PDF on mugnier.fr). Single-Guyot with a long cane and removal of the second bud ("a better solar panel"); target ~5 clusters per vine; no green harvest (he calls it a cover for earlier mistakes) but "évasivage," removal of double buds in May. Triage at the vine, not in the cuverie; a vibrating sorting table was bought in 2002 (famously used to shake out "a hundred kilos of ladybugs" in 2004). [HIGH — Nanson 2006; Jockovino 2010; Martin 2023; McInerney 2025]
- Rootstock/clones: NOT FOUND for Bonnes-Mares specifically. Frédéric prefers "clones and rootstocks that are not naturally prolific" (Jockovino). [LOW]
- Harvest order (relevant to a warm-climate future, said in 2019): "Our Bonnes-Mares is the earliest harvested parcel, it suffers from the heat, I'm obliged to do this one first. Normally it's not far from Musigny — perhaps a day." [HIGH — Nanson, July 2019]

Mugnier 2010 whole-cluster CONFIDENCE RANGE: 0% whole cluster (100% destemmed), HIGH confidence. Sources: Mugnier himself to Paul Wasserman, June 2011 ("Always 100%. Except for experimental purposes"); Kolm 2013/2014/2023; Martin 2023; McInerney 2025. The only contrary figure (Jockovino's "up to 40%") is uncorroborated and pre-dates the primary statement. If the committee raises it, the answer is that Mugnier has run stem experiments but has never integrated them in a commercial cuvée.

Why he destems — the argument in his own words (Wasserman interview, June 2011): the reasons not to use stems are "sanitary" (rot in the rachis) or "phenolic ripeness" of the stem; and on extraction generally: "Every time skins are torn they release green tannins, herbal flavors, coarse stuff." Stems are, for him, another source of exactly the coarse and herbal elements he is trying to keep out. His deeper rationale is philosophical: "Since I could never make up my mind, I decided I would vinify all the wines the same. Then that became a philosophy." Adding stems by cru or by vintage would break the rule that everything from village to Musigny is vinified identically.

#### 3.2 The rest of the régime

- Yeasts: no commercial yeast; a pied de cuve made ~10 days before harvest from cut grapes. He ran a 2006 trial of ambient vs. commercial yeast and found ambient better. [HIGH — Wasserman 2011]
- Cold soak: short, "three-day pre-fermentation maceration" (Martin 2023); 3-5 days (Jockovino/Wasserman). Fermentation ~3 weeks, starting after 3-5 days depending on tank. [HIGH]
- Vessels: the century-old open-top wooden vats in the Chambolle cuverie (unused 1950-77; re-sanded c. 2004) plus a row of temperature-controlled stainless-steel tanks upstairs; "Frédéric is not convinced that steel or wood makes too much difference, as both his are temperature controlled." Fermentation temperature allowed to rise gradually; historically hotter (up to 40°C in 1989). [HIGH — Nanson 2006; Martin 2023]
- Pigeage: "Twenty years ago I punched down vigorously"; now "I punch down at the end of fermentation … As a general rule less and less. From year to year, less and less." (2011). Older sources (Jockovino 2010: up to five punch-downs a day; Nanson 2006: pigeage preferred to remontage) describe the earlier régime. For 2010 the honest description is: gentle, with punch-downs concentrated at the end of fermentation. [HIGH]
- Chaptalisation: "less than 1º" in 2013; light or none in ripe years. 2010 figure NOT FOUND. [MED]
- New oak: "We're at 17 to 20% new wood these days, meaning that it no longer has a determining influence" (June 2011 — i.e., the 2010 régime); "I started with Jean-Marc's recommendations but I lower the proportion every year." Older references: 25-30% Allier, light toast (Jockovino 2010); ~25% for Amoureuses (Martin); ~15% today (WineWiki). For the 2010 Bonnes-Mares: 17-20% new oak, HIGH confidence. Cooper: NOT FOUND for reds (the "Jean-Marc" of the quote is not identified in the excerpt — plausibly Jean-Marc Roulot, whose family has ties to the Wasserman circle, but that is a guess [LOW]). [HIGH on %]
- Élevage: ~12 months in barrel then ~6 months in stainless "berlingots" before bottling (2011) — "noticeable progress" versus longer wood ageing. Total ~18 months. Racking as little as possible. Press wine kept separate and added, if at all, at assemblage. [HIGH — Wasserman; Jockovino]
- Fining/filtration: reds "none. Zero." (2011). Jockovino (2010) had said light egg-white fining; take the 2011 primary statement. [HIGH]
- Philosophy on balance: "You cannot reconstruct balance. You cannot, through winemaking techniques, achieve balance in what was not balanced beforehand … you run every risk of destroying harmony." [HIGH — Wasserman 2011]

### 4. The 2010 vintage at Mugnier

- Frédéric's own vintage text (mugnier.fr, "L'année 2010"): "Pure, bright, vibrant, the wines from 2010 have, like 2008 or 2001, all the qualities of late September vintages, made from grapes that ripened slowly under a mild weather. But they also have a fullness of texture that one would expect to find only in warmer vintages and this combination confers an exceptional character to the vintage. The secret is called 'millerandage' … small-size, thick skin, seedless grapes, bringing when fully mature a special quality of supple and velvety tannins. The bad side of it … is the low yield, which was specially marked in our Chambolle-Musigny ('village') vineyard." [HIGH — primary]
- To John Tilson (Underground Wine Letter, visit autumn 2011): the vintage "started very late" with slow winemaking; yields ~20% below 2009; clusters small-to-medium with thick skins and very few seeds; result "very pure with freshness." Also: "Frederic is always quick to point out that he is less interested in making great wine every year than he is in making good wine every year." [HIGH]
- To Nanson in 2019, looking back: "I think 2010 a lovely template but we see wines like that much more rarely." [HIGH]
- Harvest dates 2010: NOT FOUND for Mugnier specifically. Context: Jasper Morris's 2010 overview says most Côte de Nuits reds were picked after the wet 24 September in cool, overcast weather; Mugnier's own historical norm was "last week of September to the first week in October" (Nanson 2019). Best estimate: last days of September 2010. [LOW]
- Growing season (regional, Jasper Morris Inside Burgundy 2010 overview): severe frost below -20°C just before Christmas 2009 that killed vines along the old RN74 in Gevrey, Morey, Vosne (not reported in Chambolle); late, drawn-out flowering to mid-June with coulure and millerandage; hot dry first half of July; unremarkable August; heavy rain 8 September, hail in Santenay/Chassagne on the 12th; reds picked from ~24 September in cool conditions; crops "often below 30 hl/ha"; reds "beautifully poised … balanced triangle of fruit, tannins and acidity," 2002's purity with 1991's density. [HIGH]
- Reviews and scores for Mugnier Bonnes-Mares 2010:
  - Antonio Galloni, The Wine Advocate #198/199, 22 Dec 2011: 91-94 (barrel). "An expressive bouquet laced with dark berries, crushed rocks and graphite leads to a pretty core of dark red fruit." Drink 2020-2040. Galloni's producer note: Mugnier "views 2009 as superior to 2010 and recommends drinking 2010s sooner while cellaring the 2009s" (Cult Wines summary of the WA text). [HIGH on score/quote; MED on the paraphrase]
  - Stephen Tanzer (IWC, now Vinous): 94. "Good medium red. High-toned, liqueur-like aromas of blackberry, blueberry and smoky underbrush. Densely packed and broad, showing the sappiness and great energy of the vintage. Black raspberry fruit is complicated by iron and mocha notes. The dense finish features slowly rising persistence and surprisingly smooth tannins." (via Thatcher's Wine). [HIGH]
  - Allen Meadows, Burghound: 94 (via Thatcher's; issue/date and text not retrieved). [MED]
  - Underground Wine Letter (John Tilson, from barrel, 2011): "very well structured wine with depth and concentration, yet it retains a certain degree of finesse. It has a gorgeous perfume and lots of fruit that has great purity. This should be a wine that will drink well early and yet keep for a very long time" — "Outstanding Plus Potential." [HIGH]
  - Cult Wines lists a "highest score 92" in its own data field, at odds with the 94s above — ignore. CellarTracker community average: 94 (per Thatcher's; the CT page itself is bot-blocked). Jasper Morris, Jancis Robinson, William Kelley scores for this exact wine: NOT FOUND.
  - Bottle variation / premox: none reported. Wines bottled with natural cork; no DIAM information found.
- Drinking window opinions: Galloni 2020-2040; Mugnier himself (2025, on Musigny): "I say if the wine isn't ready to drink after ten years then perhaps I have failed." Kolm on the 2012 Bonnes-Mares suggested 2030-2060 for peak. For a 16-year-old 2010 with 0% stems, expect it to be open, floral and Fuées-like next to the Roumier. [MED]

### 5. Stories, people, connections — Mugnier

- Biography [HIGH unless noted]: Frédéric Mugnier born 1955 in Geneva, where his parents had briefly relocated from French Indochina after Dien Bien Phu; father a lawyer turned merchant banker in Paris; mother from Alsace — "a champion curler from Alsace" (Neal Martin). Degree in petroleum engineering; worked on offshore oil projects "from the North Sea to Africa" (Jockovino) — Berkeley & Stuart says Saudi Arabia [LOW]; Nanson calls him an "ex 'offshore engineer' and latterly commercial pilot." His father Jacques-Frédéric (born 1923) had sold the weakened liqueur business in 1950 and leased the vines to Faiveley; he took the Chambolle vines back in 1978 (with Bruno/Bernard Clair as régisseur, the wine sold in bulk; only the 1984s were bottled under the Mugnier label) and died in 1980. Fred, on a sabbatical in 1984 aged 29, enrolled at the Lycée Viticole de Beaune (winter 1985-86 — "a six-month crash course," Clive Coates) and made his first vintage in 1985. Because 4 ha could not support a family, he earned a commercial pilot's licence and flew for TAT (Transport Aérien Transrégional; McInerney writes "TAT," Martin writes "TAP" — TAT is correct for a French regional airline [MED]) "for half the week" into the 1990s, hiring workers for day-to-day vineyard tasks. Part of the crop was still sold to négociants until 1988. Neal Martin (2023) even speculates "whether running another career as a pilot was detrimental to the quality of the wines" of the late 1980s. The breakthrough Musigny was 1993.
- Château de Chambolle-Musigny: the bourgeois residence at the bend of the road; Roumier's domaine sits "next-door to Mugnier's Château de Chambolle-Musigny" (Nanson). The cuverie is ~100 years old, built to receive the Maréchale fruit after 1902; wooden vats original. The old cellar has "really some space here! 'You need the space to work well, and if you can work well, it's possible to make good wine.'" [HIGH]
- Clos de la Maréchale (Nuits-Saint-Georges 1er cru, ~9.5-10 ha, Mugnier-owned since the 1902 auction purchase): leased to Faiveley from 1950; the lease ended 1 November 2003 after 53 years and the domaine "s'est agrandi d'un coup de 4 à 14 hectares"; first Mugnier vintage 2004; a small Chardonnay grafting (2004-07) produces the Maréchale blanc, resurrected after old bottles were found in the cellar. This reclaim is the reason there is a modern Mugnier "domaine" at all — the Chambolle wines alone are too small to sustain a business. [HIGH — mugnier.fr; Nanson]
- The absinthe posters: the 19th-century Mugnier fortune came from liqueurs — "Absinthe Mugnier," advertised in the famous Lucien Lefèvre "Zouave" poster. [MED — Jockovino; UWL]
- Philosophy quotes worth having ready [HIGH, primary or verbatim]:
  - "Since I could never make up my mind, I decided I would vinify all the wines the same. Then that became a philosophy." (2011)
  - "I don't want to interfere in the expression of the year; cold, dry, wet, hot — it's not a problem, it's an expression of the year, it's why we put the years on the label!" (2019)
  - "I have some nostalgia for those thinner more fragile wines — ethereal and of emotion." (2021)
  - "It scares me this type of climate change. I intend to move the business to Finland if the warming increases…" (2021, having finished the 2020 harvest in August)
  - From his essay "Le Pianiste et l'Oenologue" (2008), quoting Glenn Gould: "La beauté, c'est quand on entend la musique plutôt que le musicien, et quand on savoure dans le vin le goût du raisin plutôt que la science de l'oenologue."
  - On Amoureuses vs Musigny (Kolm 2024): he attributes Amoureuses's emotional power "to the fragility of Amoureuses and the fact that no other wine resembles Amoureuses."
- Controversies: his sceptical published stance on biodynamics "raised the hackles of some proponents" (Martin 2023). He is also outspoken against green harvesting. Not a Tastevinage participant. [MED]
- Anecdote (UWL): John Tilson brought Fred a 1949 Mugnier Clos Vougeot the family had never tasted; Fred reciprocated with two of the last bottles of 1945 Musigny Blanc, slightly pétillant and "among the greatest White Burgundies I have ever tasted."
- Becky Wasserman: exporter and mentor — "I learned more about Burgundy from her than from anyone else" (Mugnier, Nov 2024, at the Musigny 1993-2016 vertical held at her Bouilland farmhouse during Les Trois Glorieuses). Paul Wasserman conducted the key 2011 winemaking interview. [HIGH]
- The Musigny release policy: Mugnier holds his Musigny back (2012 was the latest release as of 2018; the 2013 came out around 2019); vines replanted 2016-18 on one parcel (same day as Drouhin, by coincidence). [HIGH — Nanson]

## PART III — WINE (b): DOMAINE GEORGES ROUMIER, BONNES-MARES GRAND CRU 2010

### 2. Roumier's plots within Bonnes-Mares

Sizes and history [HIGH]:
- Owned: 1.3919 ha (domaine fiche: "Superficie 1.3919 hectare"). Since 2016 an additional ~0.50 ha (mostly terres blanches, at the southern end above the de Vogüé block) is farmed en métayage for an investor, bringing the exploited total to 1.89 ha. Bourgogne Aujourd'hui (2022) lists "Bonnes-Mares grand cru: 1.89 ha." Older texts give 1.45 ha (Decanter/Coates) and 1.5 ha (Burghound: "1.5 ha parcel divided into ~0.90 ha terres rouges and 0.60 ha terres blanches, slightly older vines in the latter"). For 2010 the relevant holding is the 1.39 ha (the métayage plot did not exist). Roumier is now the second-largest owner after de Vogüé.
- Origins: (i) the Quanquin dowry of 1924 — a plot "on the limestone rich 'terres blanches'" (Nanson 2009); (ii) 1952 — Georges Roumier bought a third-share of Domaine Belorgey (the rest went to Clair-Daü / Joseph Clair and Jean Ponnelle), which brought a terres rouges parcel "close to the border with Morey" (Nanson; Martin). Jean-Marie kept the Belorgey wine separate until 1962 — a 1959 bottle labelled "Ancien Domaine Belorgey" was poured for Neal Martin.
- Parcel count and soil split — sources disagree; present all:
  - Neal Martin (Vinous, Sept 2018, from Christophe): "four parcels: three located in the terres blanches on white marl that contains a larger quantity of fossilized oysters and one in terres rouges at the Morey end. … The oldest vines date back to 1902." Decanter profile (Coates-era) agrees: four parcels, three terres blanches, one terres rouges.
  - Inside Burgundy (Jasper Morris): "original holdings comprised eight separate plots"; "Originally planted at approximately 50:50 [white:red], the new acquisition shifted proportions toward white soil."
  - Domaine fiche: "Le domaine Georges Roumier possède des vignes à surfaces égales sur les 2 types de sols."
  - Burghound (quoted by Farr/BBR): 0.90 ha terres rouges / 0.60 ha terres blanches — i.e., red-dominant.
  - Kolm (Oct 2022, from Christophe): now "approximately 60-65% terres blanches versus 35-40% terres rouges. Previously, soils were equally divided."
  - Winehog cadastral listing (2018): original 1.39 ha = northern section AB 100 (0.2328), AB 66 (0.1070), AB 340 (0.1650), AB 367 (0.1608) — "predominantly white soil"; central section AB 467-470 (4 × 0.1803 = 0.7212) — "white soil at upper elevations"; plus a southern plot; new métayage plots AB 89 (0.3825), AB 298 (0.0493), AB 105 (0.0665) "in southern Bonnes Mares above the Vogüé section." Winehog: "Roumier's holdings remain entirely within Chambolle-Musigny territory."
  - Reconciliation [MED]: in 2010 the blend was roughly half-and-half by surface (domaine, Morris) or slightly red-leaning (Burghound), from four (or, counting cadastral subdivisions, eight) plots, with the terres rouges piece near the Morey line and the terres blanches pieces higher and further south. Roumier's Bonnes-Mares is thus "arguably the most representative Bonnes Mares available as it comes from multiple plots" (Jasper Morris).
- Vine age: fiche "âge moyen 40 ans"; oldest vines from 1902 (Martin); TerroirSense repeats 40 years average. Musigny/Amoureuses hold the domaine's near-centenarian vines. The domaine "ne replante pas, on repique" (replaces vines individually rather than replanting blocks). [HIGH]
- Clones/rootstock: not sélection massale; Roumier "aims for a maximum variety of clones" after an unsuccessful 2000 experiment; Charles Taylor MW records a shift from 113/114/115 to 667/777/778/828 on 161-49 rootstock (via Martin 2018). [MED]
- Farming [HIGH — Christophe to Tupinier, 2022]: mineral fertilisers reduced 1985-86 (domaine site says chemical fertilisers abandoned 1988), herbicides stopped 1989, an organic trial in 1993 failed under mildew pressure, mixed régime thereafter, gradual conversion "from 1998 onwards." "Don't say I'm organic because I'm not certified and I don't want to be. The organic label is like locking yourself into prohibitions that have only a marketing objective." On biodynamics: "I haven't tried it yet. It seems quite mysterious to me." Ploughing resumed, compost used; single-Guyot with long baguette, half the shoots removed for ventilation; north-side leaf removal. Vineyard manager since 2006: Laurent Certin.
- Yield: fiche 30 hl/ha; historically low by severe pruning (Nanson 2009).
- Vinified as two (now four) separate cuvées by soil type, blended for élevage and bottling: "La vinification donne 2 cuvées, assemblées pour l'élevage et la mise en bouteilles" (fiche). Kolm 2023: "vinified in four separate cuvées." Christophe: "I always wanted to blend the terres blanches and terres rouges together" — his father Jean-Marie had bottled them separately (the terres rouges going to importer Château & Estate) until 1988; from 1989 all four parcels are blended. [HIGH]

Direct figure for the 2010 Bonnes-Mares: NOT FOUND in any open source. Tanzer's IWC barrel notes (Jan 2012) and Burghound's producer text (Issue 45, Jan 2012) very likely record it, but both are paywalled and none of the retailer excerpts carry the stem line. Tilson (UWL, 2011) tasted the 2010s with Christophe but did not note stems.

Roumier 2010 whole-cluster CONFIDENCE RANGE: 30-50% whole cluster, most likely around 35-45%, MEDIUM confidence. Reasoning:
1. Bracketing vintages: 2009 = 60% (visitor report, May 2011); 2013 = ~35% (Kolm). Both are cellar statements from Christophe.
2. Christophe's stated rule (Martin 2018): "the riper the vintage, the most stems Roumier uses." 2010 was a cool, late, millerandé vintage with, per Christophe (UWL, Nov 2011), alcohols of only "11½-12%" (before any chaptalisation; the label says 13.5% per BBR) — i.e., distinctly less ripe than 2009, so fewer stems than 2009's 60%. His own preference for 60% whole cluster was stated in 2022, after a decade of warm vintages.
3. William Kelley's 2021 summary "30-50% stems" and the Decanter "20-50%" range bracket the plausible 2010 figure.
4. It is not zero: from 1989 onward every Bonnes-Mares has contained stems; Roumier's 2010 was noted by Meadows as "partially reduced" and cool/spicy, consistent with a meaningful stem fraction.
If forced to a single number at the table: "about a third, maybe forty percent — Christophe used sixty in the riper 2009 and thirty-five in the cooler 2013, and he goes lighter on stems in cooler years."

Christophe's own rationale (primary quotes):
- "the freshness of flavor brought by whole cluster fermentation interests me" (2022).
- Stems "normally come from the older rather than younger vines" (Martin 2018) — the old-vine fruit has the ripest, most lignified rachis.
- On the 1988 experiment (regular destemmed vs. VV with 50% stems), Martin's conclusion after four blind side-by-sides: "It transpires that the right decision was to pick earlier and use less stems. Thirty years later, the regular cuvée seems to be better, though both are stupendous" — though in the 2022 re-match the VV "showed more mineralité and complexity." A perfect dinner-table parable for the theme.

#### 3.2 The rest of the régime [HIGH unless noted]

- Sorting: in the vineyard by the pickers, then a vibrating table de tri at reception (since 2003 per Morris; "almost nothing to do there" in 2013). Picked into small bins.
- Cold soak: 14-15°C for 5-6 days on average (2022); Martin: "short pre-fermentation maceration at around 15°C"; Nanson: "a cold soak before fermentation."
- Vessels: epoxy-lined concrete and stainless-steel open tanks (Martin); "mix of stainless-steel and concrete open-top tanks" (Nanson 2009); Morris says wooden vessels increasingly replaced by inert ones.
- Yeasts: indigenous only; "pas d'enzymage."
- Cuvaison: 18-25 days (Martin); "20 à 23 jours en moyenne" (domaine); ~3 weeks (2022). Temperature capped ~32°C.
- Extraction: "The way you extract the tannins is important" — initial pigeage, then a gradual switch to remontage "to help oxygenate the wine and soften the tannins" (Martin 2018); since 2015, "frequent pump-overs and aeration, but little punch-downs" (2022). In 2010 the régime was still pigeage-led early, remontage later. On temperature as the only steering wheel (I'll Drink to That, ep. 476, Dec 2019): "If you think of driving a car, you need an accelerator and you need a steering wheel. There is nothing like that to drive a fermentation. The only way is to use the temperature control."
- Chaptalisation: none in 2023 (13.1-13.6% natural). For 2010, Christophe reported natural potential alcohols of 11.5-12% (UWL) and the released wine is labelled 13.5% (BBR) — implying a chaptalisation of up to ~1-1.5°, which was normal and legal in 2010. [MED — inference from two sources]
- New oak: "usually 20% for the village crus, 30% for the Premier Crus and around 40% to 50% for the Grand Crus. The wood is usually sourced from Vosges" (Martin 2018); Morris: "no more than 50% new wood"; Nanson 2009: "about one third of new barrels"; Decanter/Coates: ~30% max — "I want to taste the wine, not the cask"; Kolm/TerroirSense older sheet: 40% for BM; current fiche: 30% new, the rest 2-8 years old; BBR (2012 vintage): 25%. For 2010 Bonnes-Mares: 30-40% new oak, MED confidence. Cooper: NOT FOUND (Vosges forest per Martin).
- Élevage: 16 months in barrel (fiche); malolactic deliberately late (cool cellar, spring conversion); one racking the summer after harvest; press wine used only to top up.
- Fining/filtration: "ni collés ni filtrés" (fiche); Martin: fined and filtered up to 1993, not since.
- Bottling: roughly 16-18 months after harvest (spring of the second year); 2010 bottled c. spring 2012. [MED]
- Anti-fraud: every bottle now carries an NFC/RFID chip (WID app) — not applicable to 2010. [HIGH]

### 5. Stories, people, connections — Roumier

- Georges Roumier (1898-1965), from Dun-les-Places in the Morvan/Charolais, not a vigneron's son, married Geneviève Quanquin (1896-1977) of Chambolle on 14 November 1924; her dowry — Bonnes-Mares, Musigny (en métayage, bought 1978), Amoureuses — founded the domaine. Georges simultaneously ran the vineyards of Comte Georges de Vogüé; his eldest son Alain succeeded him there in 1955 and Alain's son Hervé later took vines (including some Clos de Vougeot) for his own domaine. Georges joined the Schoonmaker/Baudoin domaine-bottling pioneers in the late 1920s; first domaine bottling 1945 per Tilson, but "as late as 1984 when, for the first time, all their wines were domaine bottled" (Nanson) — the domaine site says the whole crop was bottled from 1983. Seven children; a société civile after 1965 kept the domaine intact. [HIGH — Martin; Nanson; Winehog; roumier.com]
- Jean-Marie Roumier (took over 1961; married Odile Ponnelle — hence the Ponnelle connection: Georges apparently made wine for Maison Pierre Ponnelle in the 1940s-60s, and Jean-Marie had a role there too, per the IDTT episode blurb) bought the Corton-Charlemagne land in 1968 (first vintage 1974) and Musigny in 1978; joined the 1977 purchase of Ruchottes-Chambertin from Thomas-Bassot alongside Charles Rousseau and Dr Georges Mugneret (Roumier's share farmed en métayage for Michel Bonnefond — hence the "Christophe Roumier" label). [HIGH]
- Christophe Roumier, born 1958, oenology at Dijon, stage at the Caramany co-op in 1980, joined his father in 1981 ("took charge of technical matters in 1982," Tilson; first vintage as maker 1982 per Martin — "the era known as 'B.C.' Before Christophe" ends with 1980-81), full control 1990 when Jean-Marie retired, bought out his uncles 1992; runs the domaine with his sister Delphine; keeps fit cycling and mountain-walking; "affable, sober … dressed in his work overalls — something you hardly ever see in Bordeaux" (Martin). Mentors cited on the domaine site: Henri Jayer, Jacques d'Angerville, Gérard Potel, Jacques Seysses. [HIGH]
- Philosophy — vin de garde and terroir: the domaine's line is "La signature G. Roumier, ce n'est pas un style: c'est la terre elle-même … Chaque vin doit avoir le goût de son terroir." Christophe (Nanson 2009): "based on his winemaking style" the wines are often "a little tough, young." Kolm: "As usual, expect that it will take 15-20 years to show its full potential." Decanter: "I want to taste the wine, not the cask." The Peter Muto visit note calls the 2009 BM "a true vin de garde." The often-repeated "we don't do anything" quip: NOT FOUND in any source; do not attribute it. [HIGH/MED as marked]
- On his Bonnes-Mares (Christophe to Nanson, 2009): "Has 'Chambolle' aromatics of flowers and pepper, but with the underlying density of Morey St.Denis." The domaine's tasting text: "la force d'un Morey Saint Denis à la complexité d'un Chambolle Musigny!" [HIGH]
- Prices and flipping: secondary prices reached "stratospheric levels, something that Roumier sits uneasy with and as such, does his utmost to ensure that bottles are not simply flipped for quick returns" (Martin); Musigny prices "perplex and embarrass" him; NFC chips on every bottle; a planned direct-to-consumer site to control prices (2022). [HIGH]
- The 2004 ladybug vintage: "Christophe agrees that there were ladybugs everywhere but is unconvinced that they are responsible … 'at least you always know the vintage in a blind tasting!'" (Nanson 2009). [HIGH]
- Recent expansion: 0.13 ha Echézeaux (En Orveaux, first commercial vintage 2017; shared métayage with Arnaud Mortet; Champs Traversins added by 2023); Charmes métayage superseded from 2018; the 0.50 ha Bonnes-Mares métayage from 2016. Domaine now ~12.4-12.5 ha, three-quarters leased from family. [HIGH]
- Corton-Charlemagne candour (2022): "I'm not yet a huge fan of my own wine." [HIGH]

## PART IV — CONNECTIONS BETWEEN THE TWO WINES AND TO THE DINNER

- Next-door neighbours: "The understated domaine sits on the sharp bend in the road, next-door to Mugnier's Château de Chambolle-Musigny" (Nanson). Two of the three or four most-coveted producers in Burgundy share a wall and a grand cru, and disagree fundamentally about stems. Kolm (2014): "When young, [Roumier's] wines seem more powerful and tightly-wound than those of Mugnier."
- De Vogüé link: Georges and then Alain Roumier ran the de Vogüé vineyards (Alain from 1955; Alain's family is the Hervé Roumier line). De Vogüé is the largest Bonnes-Mares owner and, like Mugnier, fully destems — so Bonnes-Mares is a climat whose biggest and smallest serious owners destem while Roumier, Dujac and d'Auvenay in the middle use stems.
- Bruno/Bernard Clair: régisseur of the Mugnier vines 1978-84 (Nanson; Martin) — and Clair-Daü was Roumier's partner in the 1952 Belorgey purchase. Bruno Clair is the third-largest Bonnes-Mares owner today.
- Jacques Seysses / Dujac (the stems evangelist): named by Roumier as a mentor; Martin calls the 2008 Roumier Musigny "Christophe Roumier's homage to Jacques Seysses." If Dujac is on the dinner list, Roumier is the bridge between the Dujac and Mugnier camps.
- Dominique Lafon: Christophe's friend and organic-farming interlocutor in the 1980s ("We were discussing it with Dominique Lafon, a friend, at a time when everyone was criticizing it").
- Becky Wasserman & Co.: Mugnier's exporter and mentor; Roumier's US channel was Château & Estate (Diageo) for decades (the terres rouges cuvée went to C&E until 1988; C&E is still listed as importer of record on the 2010 by Cult Wines) — currently Grand Cru Selections shows Roumier "current releases" [MED on current importer].
- Coopers: Roumier — Vosges oak (Martin), cooper not named; Mugnier — cooper not named. No shared cooper documented. NOT FOUND.
- Rousseau: Charles Rousseau brokered the 1977 Ruchottes purchase with Jean-Marie Roumier and Georges Mugneret. Rousseau is ~90% destem (Kelley list) — another data point for the theme.
- Confrérie des Chevaliers du Tastevin / Clos de Vougeot: both domaines donated lots to the April 2022 "Clos de Vougeot pour l'abbaye de Cîteaux" charity auction organised by the Fondation du Patrimoine and the Confrérie (Tastevinage head Arnaud Orsel), held in the Grand Cellier of the Château du Clos de Vougeot: Lot 132, Roumier Bonnes-Mares 2015 magnum "with Experience," sold €27,500; Lot 129, Mugnier Musigny 2007 jeroboam, €23,750. A second edition is scheduled for 19 September 2026 (Bourgogne Aujourd'hui). Neither domaine currently owns Clos de Vougeot (Roumier's two 1952 parcels passed to the Hervé Roumier branch in the 1990s; Mugnier sold theirs after 1949). Neither enters the Tastevinage. A light-touch anecdote: Jay McInerney skipped "the interminable dinner at the Clos de Vougeot" during Les Trois Glorieuses 2024 to sit next to Mugnier at the Wasserman Musigny vertical — perhaps not one to repeat in front of the committee.
- Shared critic verdict on the pair (Steen Öhman, Winehog): "In very few cases Bonnes Mares is mentioned as matching Musigny — aside from perhaps Domaine Georges Roumier … and in the Mugnier case the Musigny clearly the king, and the Bonnes Mares is perhaps regarded to be on more equal terms with Chambolle-Musigny Les Amoureuses." Nanson's arc on Mugnier's Bonnes-Mares: "this wine has really come on in the last decade!" (2016); "For many years I used to much prefer the Fuées here, but not any more" (2019).

Talking points for the theme, in one breath: same hill, same year, same cool 11.5-12% natural sugars and millerandé berries; Mugnier stripped every stem and used under 20% new oak so the year speaks unadorned; Roumier fermented his older-vine terres-rouges and terres-blanches lots with roughly a third to a half of their stems and 30-40% new wood, then blended, for freshness, spice and a twenty-year arc. Meadows found the Roumier "partially reduced … cool and restrained … spicier"; Tanzer found the Mugnier "surprisingly smooth tannins." That is the stems argument in two glasses.
