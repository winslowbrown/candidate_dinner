## 1. The Confrérie des Chevaliers du Tastevin

### 1.1 Founding, 16 November 1934

- The Confrérie was founded by **Georges Faiveley** and **Camille Rodier**, both of Nuits-Saint-Georges, and was "officially signed and registered on November 16th 1934." First meeting/first chapitre: 16 Nov 1934 at the **Caveau Nuiton** in Nuits-Saint-Georges. [HIGH] (Confrérie official history page; French Wikipedia; Faiveley family-history page.)
- Purpose as stated by the Confrérie today: "promoting all things Burgundian, in particular its great wines and traditional cuisine," preserving customs and folklore, developing tourism, "defending the reputation of Burgundy - and burgundy - in a spirit of Grand Cru conviviality." [HIGH] (tastevin-bourgogne.com/en/history)
- The economic backdrop is the point to make at table: the Depression had left Burgundy with unsold stock since 1929. The Faiveley family tells the founding story this way: in 1934 Georges Faiveley found a secondhand book on wine brotherhoods in the Seine bookstalls, and with his friend Camille Rodier set up the Confrérie under the slogan **"If no-one wants to buy our wines then let's invite our friends to share them with us!"** [HIGH for the quote as the Faiveley domaine's own telling; MED as literal history] (domaine-faiveley.com "A family rooted in history").
- English Wikipedia adds that the organization traces its lineage to an earlier "Ordre des Buveurs Libres de Bourgogne" (Order of Free Drinkers of Burgundy). [MED — single secondary source.]
- Legal form: a French loi-1901 non-profit association, governed by a "Grand Conseil" (board) headed by a "Grand Maître" (president); the secretary is the "Grand Connétable." The US operation is delegated to a "Grand Pilier" (Grand Pilier Général). [HIGH] (French/English Wikipedia; Massachusetts sous-commanderie history page.)
- Coat of arms designed by the Alsatian illustrator Hansi: purple field with a gold bar, a silver barrel in base and a silver tastevin in chief, surmounted by a tournament helm. [HIGH] (French Wikipedia.)
- Membership: c. 12,000 chevaliers worldwide (Confrérie's own figure); English Wikipedia gives ~12,000 as of 2016 with ~2,300 in the USA, ~75 chapters worldwide of which 33 in the US. [HIGH for the 12,000; MED for the sub-counts, which date from 2016.]
- Ranks (ascending): Chevalier → Commandeur → Officier-Commandeur → Grand Officier; chapter heads are "Grand Sénéchal"; there is also "Grand Connétable," "Grand Pilier Général" (top American rank) and an emeritus "Grand Historiographe." [HIGH] (Massachusetts page; Wikipedia.)
- Notable inductees (for conversation, not for showing off): de Gaulle, Giscard, Macron, Merkel, Kohl, Brandt, Hitchcock, Lelouch, Aznavour, Thomas Pesquet, Teddy Riner. [MED — French Wikipedia list, each footnoted there.]

### 1.2 The Château du Clos de Vougeot

- Clos created by the Cistercians of Cîteaux from donations 1109–1115; enclosure completed by 1336; the Renaissance château added in 1551 by rebuilding a chapel and outbuildings. After the Revolution the property was sold; Julien-Jules Ouvrard held it as a monopole from 1818; in 1889 his heirs sold to six Burgundy merchants — the first division of the Clos in 700+ years. Léonce Bocquet (one of the 1889 buyers) took the château and nearly bankrupted himself restoring it; he is buried there. [HIGH] (English Wikipedia Clos de Vougeot; closdevougeot.fr.)
- **Étienne Camuzet** (deputy for the Côte-d'Or, mayor of Vosne-Romanée, the Camuzet of Méo-Camuzet) bought château and clos in 1920, kept the vines, and on **29 November 1944** sold the château to the "société civile des Amis du Château du Clos de Vougeot," which granted the Confrérie's leaders a **99-year lease**. The Confrérie's own history page dates the acquisition to "after France's Liberation in 1945" and calls it "the first act" of the post-war Confrérie. So: sale 1944, installation 1945. [HIGH] (French Wikipedia with citation; tastevin-bourgogne.com; closdevougeot.fr.)
- Consequence worth stating precisely: **the Confrérie owns no vines.** The clos itself (50.6 ha, ~47.3 in production) is split among roughly 80 owners. [HIGH]
- Chapitres moved from the Caveau Nuiton to the Grand Cellier of the château after 1944. The château today keeps the medieval vat-house and four Cistercian presses, the cellar and kitchens, and "does not produce wine anymore." [HIGH] (closdevougeot.fr; French Wikipedia.)
- The château is also the headquarters ("Maison") of the **Climats du vignoble de Bourgogne** association and, in the UNESCO dossier, is one of three built "tangible traces" of the powers that shaped the region, alongside the Ducal Palace in Dijon and the Hospices de Beaune. [HIGH] (closdevougeot.fr; whc.unesco.org/en/list/1425.)

### 1.3 The Chapitres

- The Confrérie now holds **16 chapitres a year** in the Grand Cellier, each for **500–600 guests**, six courses and six wines, and inducts new members at each. [HIGH] (French Wikipedia; festivalenfrance.com confirms "16 Chapitres prévus en 2026"). The Confrérie's own page lists the seasonal cycle: "Spring, Summer, Autumn ... the Equinox, The Roses, Saint-Vincent, Saint-Hubert..." [HIGH]
- The most famous is the **Chapitre des Trois Glorieuses** on the Saturday of the third weekend of November, opening the three-day sequence: Confrérie chapitre (Saturday), Hospices de Beaune auction (Sunday), Paulée de Meursault (Monday). [HIGH] (Tastevin Canada; La Paulée Wikipedia; Wine Enthusiast.)
- Format of an evening (eyewitness, Vinispi/Antoine Debuquoy): bugles and trumpets announce the Grand Conseil; ~400 guests take gougères and Crémant; new chevaliers are called forward in groups of five and receive humorous biographical speeches before being "armed"; dinner in the cellar; the **Cadets de Bourgogne** sing drinking songs; speeches by chevaliers and celebrities, "often mockingly criticizing Bordeaux wines." [MED — single blog account, but consistent with everything else.]
- The **Cadets de Bourgogne**: the polyphonic choir that has accompanied the Confrérie since 1934; repertoire includes "Joyeux enfants de la Bourgogne" and "Le bourguignon vermeil." [HIGH] (French Wikipedia.)

### 1.4 The induction formula, the vine root, the tastevin

- Requirement: a candidate must "demonstrate their sincerity and be nominated by two sponsors who are themselves members"; the Confrérie says it values "merit as much as talent, recognizing courage, personal endeavour, scientific knowledge, a love of France and humanitarian achievements." [HIGH] (tastevin-bourgogne.com/en/intronisation.)
- Ritual words (the Grand Maître "arms" the candidate with a vine stock rather than a sword):
  > "Par Noé, père de la Vigne, / Par Bacchus, dieu du Vin, / Et par Saint Vincent, patron des Vignerons, / Nous vous armons Chevalier du Tastevin."
  English Wikipedia renders it "Through Noah, Father of Vines, Bacchus, God of Wine, Saint-Vincent, Patron of Winemakers, we make admit you as a Knight of the Tastevin." [HIGH] (French Wikipedia; Vinispi eyewitness.)
- New chevaliers receive the tastevin on its ribbon (crimson and gold, for pinot noir and chardonnay), a certificate, and — per English Wikipedia — a piece of petrified Burgundian vine root. Robes are modelled on 16th-century academic/theological gowns. [MED — ribbon symbolism and root are from English Wikipedia only.]
- The oath is sworn "sur le tastevin" (festivalenfrance description). [MED]

### 1.5 Motto and the Rabelaisian register

- Motto: **"Jamais en vain, toujours en vin"** — "Never in vain, always in wine." It is painted in the Grand Cellier. [HIGH] (French Wikipedia; Tastevin Canada.)
- The Confrérie says its rites draw on "the Rabelaisian cult of wine, which found its expression in the Middle Ages" (Tastevin Canada wording); French Wikipedia says the induction ritual is "inspiré de tout un folklore bourguignon et de pièces de théâtre comme le Malade imaginaire de Molière" — i.e., the mock-solemn Latin-doctorate ceremony of Molière's finale is the direct model for the "arming." The Vinispi account has the founders reviving "bacchanalian traditions" in the spirit of Rabelais's "truculence and good humor." [HIGH that the Confrérie itself frames things as Rabelais + Molière; the Malade imaginaire link is a nice detail to deploy.]
- Other institutions the Confrérie invented: the **Saint-Vincent Tournante** (1938), a rotating village-hosted revival of the medieval mutual-aid-society feasts of Saint Vincent, late January; a **Chambre des arts et belles-lettres** (1948) awarding a literary prize, later broadened into the Prix du Tastevin; and the **Tastevinage** (1950). [HIGH] (French Wikipedia; Confrérie history page.)

### 1.6 The Ban bourguignon

- The hand-twirling, "la la la la la lalalalalère" clap-chant that punctuates every chapitre and every Burgundian dinner. Origin: c. 1905 in a bar in the Montchapet quarter of Dijon, originally the "Ban de Montchapet," renamed "Ban bourguignon" after WWII as it spread; gesture: forearms up, wrists rotating, nine claps on the "lère" refrain. Spread by a Dijon association with the wonderful name NPSFQQA ("Ne Pas S'en Faire Quoi Qu'il Arrive"). [MED — Le Bonbon Dijon lifestyle piece; the 1905/Montchapet story is the standard local account but I found no academic source.]
- It is not a Confrérie invention but the Confrérie is its principal vector abroad; in US sous-commanderie dinners it is done at each course. [MED]

### 1.7 The Tastevinage

- Twice a year since 1950 in the cellar of the château (spring: reds; autumn: whites and Beaujolais). Blind tasting by juries of vignerons, négociants, courtiers, oenologists, sommeliers, journalists and "amateurs éclairés" — Tastevin Canada says around 250 judges. About **one third** of wines presented are "tastevinés" and may carry the Confrérie arms on the label; since a request by singer Gilbert Montagné the seal is also embossed in Braille. The 100th Tastevinage (Sept 2017) was presided by Jeannie Cho Lee MW. [HIGH] (French Wikipedia; Tastevin Canada.)
- Useful framing for Noah: none of the thirteen producers at this dinner submit to the Tastevinage — it is a label for the broad middle of Burgundy, not for the domaines in the room. [MED — I found no tastevinage listing for any of the thirteen; absence of evidence.]

### 1.8 The American Confrérie and the New York sous-commanderie

- The US branch was launched **27 March 1940 in New York** by **Jules Bohy**; a Washington DC chapter followed in 1946. French Wikipedia calls the American commanderie "la plus ancienne et la plus importante," created 1940. [HIGH] (English + French Wikipedia.)
- The Sous-Commanderie de New York existed "before 1966" and served as the model for later chapters; in 1975 "Tex" Bomba, then Grand Pilier Général, seeded the Massachusetts chapter. [HIGH] (masschevaliers.com/history.)
- **How US chapters vet candidates** (Massachusetts page, describing the model inherited from New York): three proposers from existing members; emphasis on "knowledge and interest in Burgundy wines rather than social relationships"; a candidate should have "a reasonable wine cellar from which they were willing and able to provide some of the event wines," be willing to host or co-host dinners, and be judged compatible by a committee; then "an oral examination on Burgundy wines and traditions" or, alternatively, "an evaluative lunch with senior officers" — John Sprague's 1966 lunch at Lutèce with Michel Dreyfus and "Tex" Bomba is given as the example. [HIGH for Massachusetts' own description.]
- **New York specifically** (Wall Street Journal wine-societies piece, republished by Big Hammer Wines; the WSJ author is almost certainly Lettie Teague [MED on byline]): candidates must be proposed by a member in good standing and seconded; the NY chapter administers **written exams**; wait can run to two years; **candidates host dinners for the nominating committee at restaurants or clubs, serving wines from their own cellars**; only seven or eight members are admitted per year, partly for lack of seats at the restaurants used; dues "as much as $1,800" plus per-dinner costs. Geoffrey Troy (New York wine merchant and member) is quoted: **"You need to at least bring a grand cru."** One candidate is described as having served eight bottles including 1970 Latour and 1961 Pichon Baron — i.e., Bordeaux at a Burgundy society's candidate dinner, which is the cautionary tale. The article notes requirements have hardened since the late 1970s "from informal acceptance to today's rigorous testing and trial dinners." [HIGH that this is what the WSJ reported; the dollar figure and numbers are dated — the piece is from the early 2010s.]
- A concrete recent example of the candidate-dinner custom in a US chapter: the Albany/Capital Region sous-commanderie's 2025 Chapitre and Induction Dinner (Fort Orange Club, 1 Nov 2025) was preceded "a couple of weeks before" by a **candidate interview/practice dinner at which the same wines were served double-blind** while the candidates discussed ordering and pairing. [HIGH — WineBerserkers first-hand report.] Chapter dinners there run five Burgundies across five courses, all from the chapter cellar; the 2023 dinner honoured a deceased founder with a wine "bottled for" him. [HIGH]
- What this means for Noah's dinner: the candidate dinner is the last and most personal test in the process — cellar depth, judgment in sequencing, ability to talk about each wine, and generosity (the WSJ piece is explicit that hosts are expected to pour from their own cellar and that a grand cru is the floor). The Massachusetts criteria ("willing and able to provide some of the event wines," "host or co-host dinners") are the plain-English version of what the committee is measuring. [Interpretation, but grounded in the two sources above.]
- NOT FOUND: any current public page for the New York sous-commanderie itself (its officers, dues, or a written description of the candidate dinner). The chapter appears to have no public website; everything above comes from Massachusetts, the WSJ, and other chapters.

## 2. Meursault — the village, the geology, the missing grand cru, the Paulée, and the name

### 2.1 Numbers and layout

- 2008: 394.05 ha in production (village + 1er cru), 18,536 hl, of which 18,171 hl white and 365 hl red — ~2.5 million bottles; about 98% white. 96.97 ha of premier cru (2007), 19 premier-cru climats (Wikipedia list; the doctorwine profile also says 19). No grand cru. [HIGH] (Wikipedia, citing BIVB.) Patrick Essa (Domaine Buisson-Charles) says "environ 360 hectares de vignes" and that "les premiers crus n'occupent guère plus de la surface du château Lafite à Pauillac," the 1er-cru band lying between 260 and 300 m. [HIGH as his figures.]
- The premiers crus fall in two groups: north of the village toward Volnay (Santenots, Cras, etc.) and the main band south of the village toward Puligny/Blagny (Perrières, Clos des Perrières, Genevrières, Charmes, Porusot, Bouchères, Gouttes d'Or, Ravelles, plus the Blagny climats). [HIGH]
- Etymology: the doctorwine profile gives Latin "Muris Saltus" — "rat's leap" — after a stream; the standard alternative is "murs sault," a leap/wall. [MED — folk etymology; do not lean on it.]

### 2.2 Geology and the two slopes

- The Meursault hill is cut by faulting that brings the hard Bathonian (Middle Jurassic) limestones — the Comblanchien-type "pierre de Meursault" that built the village church tower — to the surface along a line the quarries follow. Decanter's regional profile (Charles Curtis MW) describes "quarries along the fault lines underground that are clearly visible as one drives south toward Puligny," above which sit the chaumes, too thin for vines. Bill Nanson's Perrières piece: the base rock in Perrières is "a hard oolite limestone" lower down, with "more argiles and clay... with some Comblanchien limestone" higher up, and topsoil "often... no more than 20–30 centimetres... before the mother-rock." Jockovino's profile: south of the village the vineyards sit on "Bathonian and Callovian limestone and white marl," soils becoming "increasingly thin, covered with scree and underlain with friable schiste" upslope; the northeast (Santenots) sector is "clay, pebbles and iron-rich scree over a base of Bathonian limestone." Patrick Essa on Genevrières: "un sol argilo-calcaire homogène, assez peu profond – surtout en sa partie haute – datant de l'étage Bathonien... marqué par des Marnes blanches." [HIGH that these are the sources' descriptions; MED on the exact stratigraphy, which is genuinely complicated and disputed in detail.]
- The upper/lower distinction is built into the old cadastre: "Dessus" and "Dessous" (Charmes-Dessus vs Charmes-Dessous, Perrières-Dessus vs Perrières-Dessous). On the 1861 Batault-Morot classification map, "quality increases as one moves up slope"; Charmes-Dessus was entirely 1ère classe while much of Charmes-Dessous was 2e and its bottom northern part 3e classe; Rodier (1920) confirmed Charmes-Dessous was "également classé, dans quelques parties, en Première et Troisième Cuvées." Steen Öhman (Winehog): "In most case the higher part is considered superior but in vineyard located high on the slope this is not always the case." [HIGH] (winehog.org "Meursault Dessous and Dessus.")
- Character contrast, in Jasper Morris's compression: Meursault today is "rarely as buttery and nutty as in the past, but offering satisfying weight if grown on the lower slopes, or a tingling mineral character at the top of the hill." [HIGH] (insideburgundy.com/meursault.) Decanter's Curtis: lower-elevation 1ers crus (Perrières-Dessous, Clos des Perrières, Charmes, Genevrières) give "denser, more powerful wines in deeper soils," while the 300–380 m village vineyards (Vireuils, Clous, Casse-Têtes, Tillets, Narvaux, Chaumes) give "steely mineral quality and pronounced lemony fruit aroma, often with an edge of white floral notes." [HIGH]
- Skurnik's "Tale of Two Meursaults": "Compared to its neighbors to the south, Puligny and Chassagne, Meursault generally has less humid soil due to the low water table. Consequently, the crop is usually smaller, lending the power that many people see as the classic calling card of Meursault." [HIGH as quoted; this is the standard explanation of why Meursault, not Puligny, could historically cellar in deep caves.]

### 2.3 The "great village hillside" — Tessons, Narvaux, Rougeots, Chevalières, Luchets, Tillets — why these unclassified lieux-dits are treated as unofficial premiers crus

The essential source is Patrick Essa's long essay "Comprendre le vignoble de Meursault" on the Buisson-Charles site (Essa is a Meursault producer-négociant and the best-known modern commentator on the commune's parcellaire). Key passages, verbatim:

- The principle: "Le classement des parcelles, en dehors des premiers crus n'existent pas, pourtant il est d'usage de considérer certaines d'entre elles comme de véritables seconds crus, elles apparaissent sur les étiquettes avec de plus en plus d'insistance car le fait de les mentionner rend le vin plus marchand, un peu plus cher bien souvent car plus rare et sans aucun doute supérieur." He calls this labelling of village lieux-dits "un fait quasiment unique dans la Côte des blancs." [HIGH]
- The hill above the village: "Le coteau qui surplombe le village à l'ouest est le plus apte à produire des 'Meursault village' de qualité. Presque tous les lieux dits de cette zone méritent d'être mentionnés sur les étiquettes car tous possèdent un type particulier, reconnaissable dans une dégustation à l'aveugle." [HIGH]
- **Tessons** (Roulot's Clos de Mon Plaisir is here): "Le plus connu d'entre eux, car il est isolé par tous les vignerons est sans doute 'le Tessons', c'est un cru qui est chaque année très proche des premiers crus, il était placé en première cuvée au siècle précédent par le docteur Lavalle et les vignerons de la commune le situe toujours en tête des climats non premier cru avec le Limozin. Il est toujours très minéral avec un nez de silex (de pierre à fusil), il s'ouvre assez rapidement et peut se garder 25 ans sans problème lorsqu'il est bien vinifié." "Là où Tessons est tendu et racé, Limozin se montre velouté, ample et enveloppant." [HIGH] — i.e., Lavalle (1855) ranked Tessons "première cuvée," level with the crus that became premiers crus. That single fact is the heart of the "unofficial premier cru" argument.
- **Chevalières, Rougeots, Casse-Têtes** (Coche-Dury's two village lieux-dits are Chevalières and Rougeots): "Les Chevalières, les Rougeots et les Casses têtes suivent de très près accompagnés du haut des grands Charrons et de la partie la nord ouest des Tillets. Les Chevalières sont d'une finesse et d'une élégance rare en année chaude, mais on récolte toujours un peu plus tard dans ce secteur qui est un rien plus froid à cause des vents qui proviennent de la Combe d'Auxey-Duresses." [HIGH]
- **Narvaux** (William Kelley's own bottling is a Narvaux; Roulot also has one): "Ensuite se trouve un très important lieu-dit: Les Narvaux. Sol de tout premier ordre dans sa partie basse, il est capable d'égaler le Tessons à cet endroit, mais il perd de sa force au fur et à mesure de son positionnement haut sur le coteau. Très proche de l'esprit des Genevrières du haut, il n'en possède toutefois pas la puissance. C'est un vin d'une très grande longévité qui peut évoluer sur des décennies. Les murisaltiens qui en possèdent assez l'isolent systématiquement et le tienne pour un 'second cru' naturel." Note: Narvaux-Dessous sits directly above Genevrières-Dessus. [HIGH]
- **Tillets and Cloux**: "exposés plein est sur des terres hautes remises en valeur dans l'entre deux-guerres par ceux qui parmi les vignerons possédaient un double attelage équin pour y accéder... Les vins sont très fins, élégants et possèdent une distinction naturelle en année un peu chaude qui peut aller vers des sommets. Des vins souvent assez longs à se faire, tendus et possédant une énergie rare." [HIGH]
- **Luchets and Meix-Chavaux** (both Roulot): "Sous l'influence directe de la Combe d'Auxey... Les Luchets donnent des vins assez proches des Chevalières de la partie haute, mais un rien plus froid, plus tendu et avec une très belle énergie interne. Les Meix-Chavaux sont assez vastes et la meilleure partie est sans doute le bord sud du Clos des Meix-Chavaux car elle est disposée sur un sol pierreux de laverottes qui se délitent." [HIGH]
- The upper plateau: "Certains climats se trouvent placés sur le second mouvement du coteau de Meursault qui fait une sorte de replat au dessus des premiers crus avant de reprendre une forme plus pentue. Ce replat très pierreux était souvent creusé de carrière d'extraction de pierres de tailles." [HIGH] — this "replat" (bench) above the 1er-cru band is the physical reason the upper village lieux-dits behave like a second cru band rather than like ordinary village land.
- North side / Lafon's Clos de la Barre and Désirée: the north of the commune toward Volnay was historically pinot country replanted to chardonnay once AOCs (1930s) and export made "Meursault" synonymous with white; Lafon's "Désirée" is a village-labelled parcel enclaved in the 1er-cru Pétures; "La Barre et le Clos de la Barre sont proches de la seconde classe." [HIGH]
- Why the modern generation talks about this: Sarah Marsh's 2014 retrospective and Kelley's own choice to make a Narvaux are symptoms; the lieu-dit labelling of Meursault village (Roulot's five or six village cuvées, Coche's Rougeots/Chevalières, Lafon's Clos de la Barre/Désirée) is exactly what Essa describes and is unique in the Côte de Beaune whites. [MED as synthesis.]

### 2.4 Why Meursault has no grand cru — the classification story

What the sources actually say (this is contested history, and most English-language retellings are hearsay; present it as such):

- **Perrières' standing.** Dr Jules Lavalle, 1855, on Perrières: "After Montrachet, I know of no other white wine more exquisite" (Decanter's translation). [HIGH] Lavalle placed Perrières and Genevrières in his tête de cuvée/première cuvée tier. The doctorwine profile: "Premier Cru Perrières, at least, could have been classified as a Grand Cru... a historical anomaly." Winehog: "there is no Grand Crus in Meursault – although Meursault Les Perrières is close." [HIGH as opinions.]
- **The 1930s decision.** Patrick Essa is the only fetchable source that states a mechanism, and he is a Meursault insider: "Après que les différents syndicats communaux aient refusés de se mettre d'accord pour définir une aire en grand cru juste avant la seconde guerre mondiale, préférant miser sur le seul nom de Meursault, la commune s'est retrouvée avec le principal coteau blanc de Côte d'Or à délimiter selon une logique excluant les classements des autres communes et avec la volonté de créer un équilibre de production entre les zones 'premier cru', 'village' et 'régionale'." And: "Une partie du coteau orienté plein Est à Meursault n'a pas eu le classement premier cru décrété au début des années 50 du siècle précédent." [HIGH that Essa says this; MED as history.] Translation of the mechanism: when the AOC/grand-cru delimitations were being negotiated (the Côte d'Or grands crus were decreed 1936–37; premier cru delimitations followed after the war, early 1950s), the Meursault growers' syndicates could not agree on which area would be the grand cru and **chose instead to bet on the single name "Meursault,"** so the commune ended up delimiting its hillside on a logic of balancing production among 1er cru, village and regional — not on the neighbouring communes' logic of naming a grand cru.
- The "growers preferred to sell as Meursault" version that Noah has heard is therefore Essa's account (and is repeated by Jasper Morris and Clive Coates in their books in similar terms — [MED; I could not fetch the book pages; Inside Burgundy's public Meursault page does not discuss it]). The other common explanation — that Meursault sold well and the growers did not want the lower yields and higher taxes of grand cru status — is folklore I could not source: NOT FOUND in fetchable text. Do not present it as fact.
- **The modern attempts.** Decanter (Curtis): Meursault lacks grands crus "due to fragmented ownership of the vines"; a recent effort by **Domaine Albert Grivault** to elevate the **Clos des Perrières** failed for this reason, though "most authorities recognise the preeminence of Perrières." Nanson's Perrières article: the Bardet family (Grivault) compiled a dossier proposing grand cru status for Clos des Perrières "without success"; other Perrières-Dessous owners "reportedly believe their section deserves consideration," while there is no consensus between lower and upper Perrières producers — and Coche-Dury, whose holding is mostly in the higher Perrières-Dessus, "may have another view." [HIGH] — i.e., even today the commune cannot agree which part of Perrières would be the grand cru, which is a neat echo of the 1930s.
- Essa's contrarian view worth having in the pocket: "Parmi les grands crus blancs oubliés du classement des années trente, Genevrières est sans doute celui qui aurait le plus mérité d'obtenir ce titre, plus encore peut-être que Perrières si j'en juge l'homogénéité remarquable de l'ensemble du territoire qu'il occupe." And Genevrières-Dessus "a le potentiel avéré d'un grand cru... cousine fortement avec le Chevalier-Montrachet." [HIGH as his opinion.]
- Market history footnote (Skurnik): "Even as recently as the 1980s, Meursault's popularity was dwarfed by that of Puligny-Montrachet, mainly due to the prestige that the Grand Cru Montrachet bestowed. In fact, there were times when Meursault prices were even lower than village level Chablis." [HIGH as quoted.] The grand-cru gap had real commercial consequences until Coche, Lafon and Roulot changed the village's reputation.
- What Meursault does have: 0.0821 ha of Montrachet (Lafon's parcel, bought by Jules Lafon in 1918 [HIGH — Vinous]) lies in Puligny/Chassagne, not Meursault; so Lafon's Montrachet is the "Meursault grand cru" only in the sense that a Meursault domaine makes it.

### 2.5 La Paulée de Meursault and the Trois Glorieuses

- Etymology: from "poêle"/"poile" (pan), the one-pan end-of-harvest meal every domaine gave its pickers; the tradition is medieval. [HIGH] (Wikipedia; Wine Enthusiast.)
- **Jules Lafon** — married Marie Boch in 1894; bought Montrachet in 1918 and top Meursault crus; mayor of Meursault — in **1923** invited 35 friends and neighbouring proprietors to his post-harvest paulée, guests bringing bottles; this became the formal village Paulée (annual by 1932; Wikipedia says "formally organized in the mid-1920s by the Count Lafon and Jacques Prieur"). [HIGH] (Vinous Lafon Perrières vertical; Wine Enthusiast; Wikipedia.)
- It became the Monday of the **Trois Glorieuses** (Saturday chapitre at Clos de Vougeot, Sunday Hospices auction, Monday Paulée at the Château de Meursault). Wine Enthusiast dates the trio to 1933; the Hospices auction itself dates from 1859. [MED on 1933 — single source; the name is a pun on the "Trois Glorieuses" of July 1830.]
- Today: a Monday lunch for c. 700 at the Château de Meursault, vignerons bringing their best bottles; awards the **Prix littéraire de la Paulée de Meursault** — 100 bottles of Meursault to a writer. [HIGH on the format; the prize is well known but the fetchable pages did not state the 100-bottle figure — MED.] The 1941 newspaper advertisement Alice Kaplan cites (§2.6) describes it as "a literary prize of 3,000 bottles of Meursault wine... awarded to a book celebrating the glory of the land" — so the pre-war prize was apparently much larger, or Kaplan's source exaggerates. [MED]
- **Lafon's role continues**: the domaine is still the moral proprietor of the event, and Dominique Lafon was one of the young vignerons Daniel Johnnes brought to the first **La Paulée de New York** (founded 2000 by Johnnes, then at Montrachet restaurant, with permission from Meursault; Jacques Seysses of Dujac was another early participant). Johnnes: "After attending the original Paulée in Meursault in the early '90s, I understood it was a wonderful way to bring people together." [HIGH] (Wine Enthusiast; lapaulee.com.) So two Trois-Glorieuses institutions have New York descendants — the Confrérie's sous-commanderie (1940) and La Paulée de New York (2000) — and Noah's dinner sits in the first tradition while the Lafon and Dujac wines on the table connect to the second.
- "Anti-Paulée" / Jean-Marc Roulot: NOT FOUND. No fetchable source describes an "anti-Paulée" or a Roulot-led rival event. Do not use.

### 2.6 The literary claims in Noah's program note — verified

**(a) Sartre, "Explication de L'Étranger."** YES. Published in **Les Cahiers du Sud** (Marseille), **n° 253, February 1943**, alongside Éluard's "Notre année," pieces by Audisio, Tortel, Alquié and Frénaud (edition-originale.com listing; AbeBooks listing of the issue). Reprinted in **Situations I** (Gallimard, 1947); an offprint/edition dated 1946 also exists per edition-originale.com. [HIGH on 1943/n° 253/Cahiers du Sud; HIGH on Situations I; MED on the 1946 edition.] Content: Sartre's famous reading of the novel's "absurd" style — short, disconnected sentences, "a glass partition" between reader and character — and his placing of Camus's hero next to Hemingway and Kafka. [HIGH as general characterization.]

**(b) Kamel Daoud, *Meursault, contre-enquête*.** YES. First published by **Éditions Barzakh, Algiers, October 2013**; French edition **Actes Sud, May 2014**; **Prix Goncourt du premier roman 2015**; Prix des cinq continents de la francophonie 2014; shortlisted for the 2014 Goncourt and Renaudot. English: **The Meursault Investigation**, translated by **John Cullen**, **Other Press (US) 2015** (Oneworld in the UK). Premise: narrated by Haroun, younger brother of "the Arab" whom Camus never names; Daoud names him **Moussa (Musa)**. Reception: Michiko Kakutani called it "stunning"; the New Yorker ran an excerpt in April 2015. [HIGH] (Wikipedia; Dissent; LARB.) A December 2014 Facebook "fatwa" against Daoud by Salafist Abdelfatah Hamadache is widely reported but was not in the fetched pages — [MED].

**(c) Is Camus's "Meursault" the wine?** The scholarly source is **Alice Kaplan, *Looking for The Stranger: Albert Camus and the Life of a Literary Classic* (University of Chicago Press, 2016)**, and her Chicago press-blog post "on the origin of Meursault" (Feb 2017). Her findings, verbatim where possible:
- The precursor: in *La Mort heureuse* (written 1936–38, unpublished until 1971 in the Cahiers Albert Camus), the hero is **Patrice Mersault** — no "u." [HIGH] (Wikipedia; Kaplan.)
- The manuscript: "Camus writes his character's name without a 'u' throughout" the surviving manuscript of *L'Étranger*; the "u" appears only in the published text. [HIGH] (Kaplan.)
- The wine: "For any French reader, that name can only signify the delicious and expensive white Burgundy wine." [HIGH] (Kaplan.)
- The dinner-party story: "Some Camus experts claim he thought of the name change at a dinner party where he was served an especially good bottle of the Burgundy wine." [HIGH that Kaplan reports it; she does not endorse it.]
- **The Paulée prize connection**: "Every November, a literary prize of 3,000 bottles of Meursault wine was awarded to a book celebrating the glory of the land. An ad for the prize appeared in the French press in November, 1941, as Camus was putting finishing touches on his novel." The Scottish Daily Mail summary of Kaplan adds that Camus may have changed the name "jokingly" to improve his chances. [HIGH that Kaplan makes this suggestion; she frames it as a possibility.] This is the single best link between the wine and the novel: the prize is the Paulée's prize, and the Paulée is Jules Lafon's creation — so there is a defensible line from Comte Lafon to Meursault the character, via a 1941 newspaper advertisement.
- **"Meurt + sault/soleil"**: Kaplan notes that the added "u" makes the first syllable "signify death — *meur*" (from *mourir*), fitting a book that ends at the guillotine, and that the spelling simply sounds more French. The "mer + sault/sot" (sea + fool) reading is also reported. The clean "meurt + soleil" decomposition circulating online (e.g., secouchermoinsbete.fr) is folk etymology; its own commenters flag that Camus used "Meursault" as a pseudonym before the novel and that none of this is confirmed by the author. [HIGH that these are interpretations; the "death/sun" reading is unattested by Camus.]
- **The publisher theory**: Kaplan: "Is it possible that they took matters into their own hands... and changed Mersault to Meursault on publisher's page proofs?" — in spring 1942, when Camus was ill with tuberculosis and Gallimard was proofing in Paris — and she admits she has "no way to prove it." [HIGH]
- Bottom line for the program note: (1) Camus never explained the name; (2) the wine association is unavoidable for a French reader and Kaplan takes it seriously; (3) the strongest documentary hook is the November 1941 advertisement for the Meursault (Paulée) literary prize; (4) "meurt/soleil" is a reading, not an etymology; (5) the manuscript says Mersault. Cite Kaplan 2016.

## 3. The four vintages

### 3.1 2010 — Côte de Nuits reds

**Growing season (Jasper Morris, Inside Burgundy 2010 overview; Decanter guide; Sarah Marsh MW):**
- "The winter was cold, exceptionally cold, with temperatures dropping below -20°C shortly before Christmas" 2009; vines were killed in the same frost-pocket sites as in 1985, on western-facing lower slopes where cold air settled; Old Vine Notes estimates the December 2009 frost cut yields by 30% or more, chiefly at village and Bourgogne level. [HIGH]
- Late flowering "through the middle of June, with some coulure and a lot of millerandage"; early July "exceptionally hot and dry" (2003 comparisons), then "the weather weakened in the second half of the month and August never really happened." Cool wet August with mildew and rot pressure. [HIGH]
- September: rain on the 8th, improvement 11th–12th, then a Sunday-night storm with "heavy hail in Santenay, stretching into Chassagne and the top end of St-Aubin." Whites picked "during the week of September 13th" to the 23rd; reds "through late September–early October" (Decanter: "underway by the last week in September and continued into early October"). [HIGH]
- Yields: "often below 30hl/ha"; Morris's key line: "a 2009-size crop would have rotted rather than ripened." F+R/Vinfolio: 30–40% below average — "a typical producer who produced 13 barrels of something in 2009 probably only has 7 barrels this year." [HIGH]

**Style and consensus:**
- Morris: "Beautifully poised wines in 2010, very classical, with much more red fruit than black, notably cherries and raspberries, and very few green notes." And, revising upward over time: "The reputation of the 2010 vintage has continued to grow, so that for red wines it is now seen as perhaps the best vintage for a generation, excluding 2005." [HIGH]
- Decanter: "The wines don't have the opulence of the 2009s, but they do have intensity, limpidity, and structure, and the best will be very long-lived." "The Côte de Nuits is exceptional in 2010." Decanter vintage-guide rating 4.5/5 for Côte d'Or red. [HIGH]
- Sarah Marsh MW (Decanter): "Gevrey-Chambertin was consistent, with fine-textured tannins and energy coupled with elegance"; Chambolle "delightful, fresh and lucid"; Vosne "charm: less power and more elegance." On stems: growers used "ripe stalks" in 2010 to add "extra finesse and dimension." [HIGH] — directly relevant to the stems flight: 2010's small, thick-skinned, millerandé berries and long hang-time gave lignified rachis, and it is generally treated as a whole-cluster-friendly year.
- Jancis Robinson vintage chart: "A return to the high acid norm after 2009, and some very good quality – especially in the Côte de Nuits – but volumes up to a third lower than average." [HIGH]
- F+R: "freshness similar to the 2008s, a concentration reminiscent of the 2009s and a fruit character similar to 2002"; "In the Côte de Nuits... we really are talking about a near-perfect vintage." [HIGH as quoted.]
- Old Vine Notes' survey of growers: a "connoisseur's vintage"; one grower: "2009 is a universal vintage... 2010 is more likely to be appreciated by [sophisticated collectors]"; Michel Lafarge compared it to 1962. [MED — blog, but reporting named growers.]
- Burghound (Allen Meadows): consensus is that he rated 2010 very highly for the Côte de Nuits, with "is 2010 better than 2009?" as his framing question (Vinfolio). Verbatim Burghound vintage text: NOT FOUND (paywalled). Neal Martin, Tanzer verbatim: NOT FOUND.

**Drinking in 2026:** 16 years on, village and most 1ers crus are open; grands crus from Gevrey/Morey/Vosne are in an early plateau. Morris's early note that 2010 reds "display immediate accessibility while maintaining aging potential" has held. [MED — inference from Morris + Decanter drinking windows; no 2025–26 retrospective fetched.]

### 3.2 2011 — Côte de Beaune whites

**Season (Morris; Jeannie Cho Lee MW in Decanter; Jancis):**
- "A fine spring was ushered in from the middle of March"; exceptionally hot April; "The first flowers were seen as early as May 10th, almost exactly the same as in 2007." Late June heat "flirting with 40°C." July "cooler and wetter than usual"; heavy rain 3–8 and 12–15 August; "The heavy rain on Friday 26th was one storm too many," but picking then proceeded dry. Minor hail on Corton-Charlemagne (20 May), Gevrey (23 July), Puligny; devastating hail in Rully (12 July). [HIGH]
- Harvest: "the earliest since 2003 for many growers"; **Domaine Leflaive began 25 August and finished six days later, its earliest harvest in history**; ~110 days flowering-to-harvest despite the early date. Jancis: "one of the earliest ever in Burgundy," picking from 25 August. [HIGH]
- Yields: "average to slightly higher than average" (Cho Lee). Ripeness: "Most growers had to chaptalise to get 12% to 12.5% alcohol levels" (Cho Lee); Jancis: most chaptalised, wines averaging 13–13.5%. [HIGH]

**Style and consensus:**
- Morris: whites have "the fresh aspect of 2007 but a little more weight"; reds show "thicker skins... greater density of fruit and more structure," with parallels to "fine and fresh 2002 reds." His ten-year update: "2011 whites are probably at optimum drinkability" — balanced but with a "slight lack of personality." [HIGH]
- Cho Lee: "2011 is a vintage for those who prefer elegance and purity over power and concentration"; "seductive delicate flavours, purity of expression and delightful aromatics" but slim; "The 2011 whites are not for long-term cellaring." She singles out producers "like Arnaud Ente and Guy Roulot, [who] crafted Meursaults that have the focus." Jérôme Flous (Faiveley): "Our best whites are pure and aromatic." [HIGH] — note the direct mention of Ente and Roulot as 2011 successes.
- Decanter vintage guide: Côte d'Or white 3/5, "Drink soon"; "Vintage quality hinged on harvest timing... The best domaines got it right, delivering wines with freshness and precision." [HIGH]
- Jancis (2021 retrospective, "2011 burgundies – looking good"): found reds and whites "every bit as successful," scored top wines 19–19.5 ("very rare" for her), and on the feared greenness: "So did we find this mysterious greenness in the wines? Absolutely not." She notes 2011s remain "cheaper than any red burgundy vintage up to and including 2019." [HIGH]
- Nanson's Perrières piece calls Lafon's 2011 Perrières "the most direct, lithe and mineral" of its vertical. [HIGH]

**Drinking in 2026 / premox:** at 15 years a 2011 Meursault from a top domaine is at or just past peak; the vintage's moderate concentration and early harvest argue for drinking, not holding. Premox: 2011 falls after the worst years (1995–2007) and after most domaines' changes (earlier bottling, more SO2, DIAM at Lafon/Roulot/Leflaive-era decisions), but it is not immune; Old Vine Notes reported in late 2011 that "premature oxidation issues persisted unresolved across most producers" for the 2010 whites, so a 2011 is a moderate-risk bottle — sensible to have a backup. [MED — inference; no 2011-specific premox survey fetched.]

### 3.3 2014 — whites (and Côte de Nuits reds)

**Season (Morris; Sarah Marsh MW; Wine Spectator; F+R; Jancis):**
- Mild winter, early start; flowering "in settled conditions" (Jancis). **28 June hail**: "Several waves of hail once again trashing the vineyards of Meursault, Volnay, Pommard and Beaune" (Morris); F+R: "destroyed 10–90% of crops from Beaune to Meursault"; also hit Echezeaux/Clos de Vougeot and the Mâconnais around Chardonnay/Lugny. Third consecutive hail year for the Côte de Beaune (2012, 2013, 2014). Because it came pre-véraison, quality of surviving fruit was unaffected (Marsh). [HIGH]
- July–August "warm and sunny days alternated with a mix of showers and storms"; cool mid-August; uneven véraison. **Drosophila suzukii**: "The vinegar fly, Drosophila suzukii, which had been around for the last few years but in 2014 became a plague" (Morris) — it pierces healthy fruit to lay eggs, triggering acetic (sour) rot; responses were "meticulous triage in the vineyard" and "early sulphuring"; some Côte de Nuits growers picked threatened plots earlier than intended. Winelens: it hit Pinot "in lower-altitude, humid sites," raised acidity and introduced vegetal characters. Wine Spectator's contemporaneous report did not mention it and instead quoted the BIVB's "Very rarely have we seen such healthy leaves." [HIGH on suzukii as the reds' problem; note the discrepancy between the trade's 2014 press and later critical consensus.]
- September: "consistently warm, tempered by a coolish northern breeze" from "Le Grand Départ" on the 13th (Wine Spectator: picking began 12 Sept in the Côte d'Or); storms 19–20 Sept; most picking done by the 22nd; "the relative coolness of the late season" gave a wide picking window (Morris). BIVB: "September brought marvelous weather. Light, warm northerly winds, warm days, cool nights." [HIGH]
- Yields: tiny where hailed (Meursault 1ers crus, Beaune, Volnay, Pommard), "fairly generous" in Puligny/Chassagne, "rather below par in most red wine villages" (Morris). Alcohols 13–13.5% without chaptalisation (Marsh). [HIGH]

**Whites — consensus:**
- Morris: "the most consistent for decades," "concentrated very fresh fruit and relatively high acidity"; "the most consistently fine white wine vintage for a generation"; and at ten years: his opinion "has not only held up but if anything gained ground... the wines seemed to be getting younger and fresher, rather than evolving, and only now are they coming into their full majesty" — 8 five-star wines out of 34 in his 10-year tasting, "a much higher percentage than I usually award." [HIGH]
- Sarah Marsh MW (World of Fine Wine / Club Oenologique, 2020–21 retrospectives): "Poised. An elegant style with an excellent balance of fresh acidity and perfectly ripened fruit"; "an haut couture vintage. Simple and beautifully constructed"; "Like a well-cut dress, they were deceptively simple, a few deft lines yielding something undeniably stylish"; "just enough of everything and nothing in excess"; "Meursault has typically generous, spicy and flowery characters." But: "Based on this tasting, I am not convinced 2014 will make old bones"; "While the 2014s have the balance and pH for decent ageing, their fine-boned structure makes them slightly more fragile"; "it does not seem to be a vintage for extensive ageing." She recommends drinking top 1ers crus now and over the next six to eight years, Bâtard/Chevalier 2024–2034. [HIGH]
- Decanter guide: Côte d'Or white 5/5, "Keep"; "The great sites delivered excellent wines of minerality, fruit intensity and staying power." Henri Boillot (F+R): "2014 has the acid and fat of 2008 and the finesse and precision of 2010"; "One of the signatures of 2014 is energy from the ripe acids." [HIGH]
- **Premox in 2014 whites**: Marsh found "four of the 14 premiers crus from Meursault, Chassagne, and Puligny were showing oxidative characters, well before they should" in one tasting and "three or four" in another, with "some disappointments from top-end premier cru wines [that] faded too quickly"; DIAM-sealed wines (Bouchard, Olivier Leflaive) were "super-fresh." Morris, by contrast, calls 2014 "a low risk candidate for premature oxidation" because of its fresh fruit and acidity. Her practical notes: Carillon's "generous 50mg/l of free sulfur," Olivier Leflaive and Bouchard bottling with 800–1,100 mg/l CO2. [HIGH] Net: moderate residual risk, closure- and domaine-dependent; a 12-year-old 2014 is still inside the window most critics give it.

**Reds — consensus (Côte de Nuits):**
- Morris 2014 overview: reds "a little less consistent," especially in the Côte de Nuits — some with "an under-ripe feel to the wine, and a lack of density" from early picking under suzukii pressure; "some very pleasing reds, though others are a little lacking in weight and energy"; "there will be few exceptional bottles." At ten years: "much more complicated" than the whites; only two five-star reds, both Côte de Beaune; "occasional signs of dry tannins, perhaps a post-hail effect, and a few bacterial issues, more often from the Côte de Nuits"; the reds are "typically sound, reasonably structured," "beginning to be open for business," with "more weight to them than 2013, but perhaps fewer moments of excitement." [HIGH]
- Decanter guide: Côte d'Or red 4/5; "less voluptuous and rich than 2009 or 2012, but also riper than 2008"; "Many are forward wines for drinking over the next eight to 10 years, as well as superb wines from top vineyards that should age well." Jancis: "Wines are relatively light." Étienne Grivot (to Wine Spectator, 2014) compared the young reds to 1989. Winelens ranks it "better than 2011, comparable to the more acidic 2013, slightly behind the more complete 2012." [HIGH]
- Relevance to the stems flight: 2014's cool, showery summer meant rachis often did not lignify fully, and suzukii forced some early picking; whole-cluster in 2014 is therefore the harder case — the year in which stem inclusion could tip into greenness — whereas 2010's tiny, late-ripened, thick-skinned crop is the easy case. That is the contrast the flight is built to show. [MED — synthesis; supported by Morris's under-ripeness remarks and the general lignification literature in §4.]

### 3.4 2019 — whites

**Season (Nanson, Burgundy-Report "2019 White Burgundy – Part 2"; Lay & Wheeler; Decanter; Morris):**
- Frost: Lay & Wheeler gives **4 April and again 15 April**; Nanson "late March/early April frost reached -4°C"; losses of 30–50% for some producers, worst in Saint-Aubin and Chassagne (L&W), and mostly on lower slopes. Noah's "April 5" is within a day of the L&W date; use "the night of 4–5 April." [HIGH on early-April frost; MED on the exact night.]
- Flowering 4–20 June in wet, windy weather → coulure and millerandage (Nanson); L&W: "undersized berries producing minimal juice."
- Heat: end-June heatwave with temperatures "exceeding 40°C" pre-véraison (Nanson) / "42°C" (L&W), then a cooler week with cool nights; a second late-July heatwave (Morris's "trio of hot dry vintages" framing). Drought: low reserves after 2018, "only scattered light showers" in August–September. Minimal disease. [HIGH]
- Harvest: early September (Nanson). Yields "exceptionally low — comparable to the 2016 frost year"; Puligny averaged 25 hl/ha; one grower: "my lowest volume harvest in 30 years." Decanter: frost, poor flowering and heat "each eliminated roughly 10% of the harvest." PYCM: frost cut 30–70% depending on parcel, fruit maturity "exceptional" at 13.7–14.7° potential with "good acidities." [HIGH]
- Alcohol: "Most wines reached 14%+" (Nanson); Charles Ballot: "not much is under 14°." [HIGH]

**Style and consensus:**
- Morris: "The second of the trio (to date!) of hot dry vintages, 2019 is currently considered as the most successful for reds except where alcoholic degrees went off the scale, while appreciation of the whites depends on whether or not the ripe sunny style suits your palate." [HIGH]
- Nanson: "purity allied to concentration"; "you rarely sense the level of alcohol in these wines"; only 4 of 47 domaines showed obvious alcohol; "yellow citrus fruit"; needs "cellar time" unlike the easy 2018s. **Léa Lafon**: juice "super balanced" despite high sugars, "fresher fruit in 2019 vs 2018 despite the higher alcohols." Charles Ballot: "I imagined wines that could be much richer." [HIGH]
- Neal Martin (Vinous, via L&W): Côte de Beaune chardonnays "unexpectedly offer freshness and richness that were once thought to be mutually exclusive." [HIGH as quoted by L&W.]
- Decanter guide: Côte d'Or white 4/5, "Keep"; "From cold to hot, a vertiginous year that produced surprisingly svelte wines"; "yet another very good vintage for white Burgundy, albeit small," with "a hedonistic" character. [HIGH]
- William Kelley (Wine Advocate) verbatim: NOT FOUND (paywalled; no retailer excerpt fetched). Burghound verbatim: NOT FOUND.

**Drinking in 2026 / premox:** at seven years the 2019 whites are in their first drinking window; the concentration and low yields argue for patience for 1ers crus from the best hills. Premox: the structural risk factors in 2019 are the opposite of 2014's — higher pH and alcohol, lower malic — but the era's technical changes (DIAM or wax at Lafon/Roulot, earlier bottling, better SO2 management, Lafon's hyperoxidation regime adopted on Denis Dubourdieu's advice from 2009) make catastrophic premox rare; the more likely fault is precocious evolution in warm-year wines. [MED — inference from the sources above.]

## 4. The whole-cluster debate in Burgundy — the frame for "The Importance of Being Stemmed"

### 4.1 History and the two camps

- Whole-bunch vinification was the historical default everywhere (destemming by hand was laborious); the Burgundian swing to destemming in the second half of the 20th century is inseparable from **Henri Jayer** (1922–2006), who "was known for always destemming his grapes in contrast to habits of other growers in Burgundy," to avoid harsh green tannins, and who pioneered the cold pre-fermentation soak (~10°C for 1–4 days) and refused filtration; he transferred his vines to nephew Emmanuel Rouget in 1996 and made his last vintage in 2001. [HIGH] (Wikipedia.) His pupils and admirers — Méo-Camuzet (where he was métayer and mentor to Jean-Nicolas Méo), Rouget — carried the destemming orthodoxy; Rousseau, Mugnier, Grivot and most of Vosne destemmed fully for their own reasons.
- Roy Richards (UK merchant, Richards Walford) captures the ideological era: "As a disciple of late Henri Jayer, I followed his mantra that stalks led to green tannins"; now "I no longer have an ideological view... it is rather more complicated than I used to believe"; "Burgundy is no longer such a marginal climate"; "Stalks lend finesse and floral perfume to wines that might otherwise be butch." [HIGH] (Jamie Goode, wineanorak.)
- Jasper Morris on why the pendulum swung back: "Clearly, in Burgundy at the moment there is a tendency to move towards stems," for two reasons: "(1) Henri Jayer, who hated stems, is dead. (2) With global warming, stems ripen more often." And: "There is an immense difference in flavour profile from all the people who do use stems... You have to look at the techniques involved. It gets very complicated." [HIGH] (wineanorak.)
- The "stems camp" of traditional flagship domaines: **DRC** (Jeremy Seysses: "Whole-cluster fermentations were once rare, though used in a number of traditional flagship Domaines (e.g., DRC)" while "most other Burgundian wineries historically destemmed 100%"), **Dujac** (Jacques Seysses from 1968: "100% whole-cluster... fermented in 100% new oak" per Wikipedia; Jeremy: "We use whole cluster in high percentage (averaging 85%) almost every year" [2018]; 65–100% depending on cuvée [Goode]), and **Leroy** (Lalou Bize-Leroy's Domaine Leroy is universally described as 100% whole cluster — [MED; no fetched page states it]). Also Domaine de l'Arlot under Jean-Pierre de Smet (ex-Dujac), Ponsot, Prieuré-Roch. [MED on the wider list.]
- The destemmers: **Rousseau** (Nanson, Dec 2016: "About 20% whole clusters were incorporated for all range in this vintage [2015]" — i.e., Rousseau is mostly-destemmed with a modest, vintage-dependent whole-cluster fraction; earlier decades were essentially 100% destemmed — [MED on the historical figure; per-wine file should carry the year-by-year numbers]); **Mugnier** ("100% de-stemmed" — Becky Wasserman profile; his credo, per Wasserman: "In winemaking, Fred is seeking the absence of Fred"); **Comtes Lafon** reds ("100% de-stemmed" — Wasserman); **Méo-Camuzet** and **Grivot** (Jayer-school destemmers — [MED; Méo's Jean-Nicolas is interviewed on the IDTT "100% Whole Cluster" episode as a destemmer voice]); **Roumier** sits in between (Christophe Roumier uses a variable, often ~20–30% whole-cluster fraction depending on vintage and cuvée — [MED; the per-wine file should carry Nanson/Meadows figures with dates]); **Liger-Belair** likewise variable. [MED]
- The 2007 harvest post from Nanson ("whole clusters anybody?") records a domaine fermenting old-vine Vosne "with a high percentage of whole clusters" while destemming the young vines from the same vineyard in order to compare — the experimental, plot-by-plot approach that has become the norm. [HIGH]

### 4.2 The technical arguments (with sources)

- **Potassium and pH.** Stems are rich in potassium; Pax Mahle: "The inclusion of stems adds potassium to the must," which "can change the buffering capacity of the juice," giving an "increase in the pH of the finished wine." Seysses: "It tends to make the pH rise." Goode: "a slight rise in pH," increasing Brettanomyces susceptibility while improving mouthfeel. [HIGH] (GuildSomm/Kelli White; wineanorak.)
- **Tannin.** Stem tannins add structure but of a different grain: Taras Ochota's image — "The bigger stones I find are from the skins, and the finer particles from stalks... The matrix together hopefully creates a mouthfeel that is long and wide." Goode: a "grippy, spicy tannic edge" in youth that "can sometimes resemble structural new oak presence." Seysses on the negative case: "Bad vegginess. Tomato leaves. Broccoli. Green bean smells. Harsh tannins." [HIGH]
- **Aromatics — the 'sous-bois'/spice register.** Seysses: whole cluster gives "More expressive, jump-out-of-the-glass aromatics; more depth, complexity, and longevity"; "We have the feeling that we get greater complexity and silkier tannins with whole cluster fermentation"; the goal is "not... 'taste of stems' or carbonic fermentation smell" but "the added complexity, texture, and freshness." Ochota: "more perfumed, spicy, forest floory, eloquently lifted." Goode's catalogue of youthful stem aromas: "broccoli, soy sauce, compost, mulch, forest floor, herbal, green, black tea, cedar, menthol, cinnamon," which "often resolve with bottle age." [HIGH]
- **The carbonic element.** With uncrushed berries inside intact clusters, a fraction (Tim Kirk estimates ~20%) begins intracellular fermentation, releasing sugar slowly; Mark Haisma: "Whole bunch for me is about controlling the ferment, slowing it down, with slow release of sugar." Mathieu Lapierre's corrective: "carbonic is always partial... To create CO2, you need juice fermenting at the bottom of your vat." [HIGH]
- **Temperature and physical effects.** Michel Bettane: "In small vats like those used in Burgundy, stems are useful because they drain juice homogeneously"; "They keep fermentation temperature one or two degrees lower"; "With large tanks it's impossible to keep stems because they make the cap too resistant"; "In 1983, curiously the whole bunch-made burgundies were less flawed by rotten berries." Seysses on loading: destemmed fruit at the bottom, whole clusters on top, by gravity, no crushing; foot-treading "Not all that important. More important is gravity-loading." [HIGH]
- **Ripeness/lignification of the rachis.** Seysses's target: lignified "Up to the knuckle is what I usually shoot for." Pax Mahle's caution: "Lignification is important, but not how most people think... The stems are bright green, even when they are lignified appropriately" — lignification is cellular hardening, not browning, and "could be affected by shortened ripening." Lapierre: "The stems will be lignified when the berry is sexually ripe." Nick Mills's field test: "If we can chew the stems through then we'll put them in." Ochota: "More spice comes from more lignification." Haisma: "I normally find a strong correlation between the better sites and the amount of stem/whole bunch I am able to use... The stems from the best sites are generally cleaner and richer in character." [HIGH]
- **Colour and alcohol.** Stems absorb pigment (Eben Sadie: "I'll lose some colour to gain freshness and purity"); water in stems and slower sugar release modestly lower final alcohol (Eric Texier's claim; Goode). [HIGH as claims.]
- **Site-specificity.** Seysses: "Some terroirs don't seem to do so well with whole cluster... it doesn't mesh well" — Dujac's own practice of destemming more in certain cuvées. His warning about fashion (2018): "It has become currently very fashionable in Burgundy and beyond"; "There are too many caricatures of whole-cluster wine in the market"; and on a consulting client's local grapes, "Anything over 10% whole cluster was hard, tannic, and green to the point of undrinkable." [HIGH]
- **2010 vs 2014 suitability** — see §3.1/§3.3: 2010 gave ripe stalks (Marsh) and is the easy case; 2014's cool wet summer and forced early picking under suzukii is the hard case. [MED synthesis]
